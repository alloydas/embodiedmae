% ABSTRACT
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{abstract}
Editable, biologically interpretable 3D models of field-grown crops are essential for high-throughput phenotyping, \emph{in silico} breeding trials, and agricultural digital twins. However, recovering these models from field sensors remains difficult because plant leaves are thin and slender, neighboring plants in a row heavily occlude one another, and routine field campaigns typically capture only a single photograph, a noisy depth map, or a partial point cloud per plant. In this regime of slender geometry under heavy occlusion, both standard 3D point-cloud completion and Chamfer-based evaluation break down. To overcome these challenges, we introduce \method{}, a multimodal masked-autoencoder framework that recovers the complete procedural representation of a plant---the generator parameters specifying leaf count, insertion heights, blade angles, and surface geometry---from any subset of RGB, depth, and point-cloud observations in a single forward pass.

We pretrain \method{} on 15{,}000 procedurally generated plants per species (maize and sorghum), fine-tune it on simulated crop rows where neighboring leaves bend realistically upon contact, and distill a full-modality teacher into single-sensor student models. Cross-modal distillation cuts sorghum parameter error from point clouds alone in half and reduces cross-modal generation loss by 30\% in sorghum and 44\% in maize. Fine-tuning on simulated rows removes 92\% (maize) and 85\% (sorghum) of the reconstruction error introduced by occluding neighbors. Through controlled ablations across both species, we establish a key architectural principle: exposing ground-truth procedural parameters to the encoder degrades learned sensor representations, whereas using them strictly as a reconstruction target helps maize while hurting sorghum. We also show that Chamfer distance and linear probes on frozen representations rank sensor modalities differently on thin foliage, motivating our adoption of frozen-latent probes as the primary metric for architectural decisions. Finally, on crop rows constructed from real plants of both species, zero-shot transfer degrades on plants taller than the synthetic training range, but fine-tuning on 75\% of the real plants brings the held-out test set to a 1.6\% median height error and predicts leaf count within one leaf on 70\% of maize captures---running in milliseconds per plant rather than minutes or hours. We will release \dataset{}, our 30{,}000-plant, two-species benchmark pairing multimodal field captures under simulated row occlusion with exact procedural ground truth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Accurate 3D models of individual crop plants allow breeders and agronomists to measure morphological traits---such as leaf angle, internode length, and canopy structure---at scale, while powering agricultural digital twins for \emph{in silico} breeding trials~\citep{hadadi2025procmaize, maizefield3d2025}. Procedural representations are uniquely well suited to these applications: instead of storing millions of unstructured points, a procedural model represents a plant as a compact set of biologically meaningful parameters that a generator expands into complete, editable, simulation-ready 3D geometry~\citep{prusin1990abop, floraforge2026}. A procedural plant requires only a few kilobytes of storage instead of megabytes, maps directly to agronomic traits, and can be cleanly edited or perturbed in ways that raw meshes and point clouds cannot. From a computer vision perspective, recovering such models from field sensors also poses a fundamental 3D reconstruction problem: inferring a parametric model of a thin, heavily occluded object from sparse, partial multimodal observations.

Despite their utility, existing methods for recovering procedural plant models rely on clean, high-density observations of isolated plants. Manual specification takes hours per plant~\citep{floraforge2026}; staged pipelines combining vision-language models with differentiable surface fitting reach 5.4\,mm median accuracy, but require a complete terrestrial laser scan of an isolated plant and roughly ten minutes of processing per instance~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize}; per-scene optimization fits parameters against multi-view imagery~\citep{cropcraft2026}; and existing feed-forward networks assume clean, unoccluded point clouds~\citep{demeter2025, ghrer2025paramplants}. High-throughput field phenotyping presents the exact opposite setting: thousands of plants are imaged daily using whichever single sensor or partial sensor suite is practical on a ground rover. Moreover, crop plants consist of slender organs (a maize leaf is only centimeters wide and millimeters thick, yet up to a meter long) tightly interleaved with neighboring plants in the row. In our field simulations, roughly one third of the target plant's visible pixels and two thirds of the captured 3D points belong to adjacent neighbors. This combination violates the core assumptions of standard 3D completion benchmarks, which focus on compact, mostly convex objects~\citep{yuan2018pcn,yu2021pointr}, and exposes the insensitivity of Chamfer distance to thin sheets~\citep{wu2021dcd, song2026structural}. While recent occlusion-aware plant completion methods recover missing geometry, they output unstructured point clouds rather than editable procedural models~\citep{lee2026occ}. To our knowledge, no prior approach recovers a complete, re-renderable procedural plant from a single field observation under row-neighbor occlusion.

\method{} makes this inverse problem tractable by using the procedural generator itself as both a data engine and a structural prior. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, we pretrain a four-stream masked autoencoder~\citep{he2022mae,bachmann2022multimae,mizrahi2023fourm,dong2025embodiedmae} over RGB images, depth maps, point clouds, and tokenized procedural parameters across 15{,}000 generated plants per species (maize and sorghum). Because random subsets of tokens are masked across modalities during pretraining, a single network learns to infer missing streams from any available sensor combination. We then distill a full-modality teacher~\citep{hinton2015distillation} into single-sensor students and fine-tune the model on simulated crop rows whose neighboring leaves bend naturally on contact. Cross-modal distillation cuts sorghum parameter error from a point cloud alone in half and reduces overall cross-modal generation loss by 30\% in sorghum and 44\% in maize, while row fine-tuning eliminates 92\% (maize) and 85\% (sorghum) of the reconstruction error caused by neighboring plants.

Our controlled ablations also reveal an important architectural insight for any multimodal system that has access to ground-truth generative parameters during training: allowing the encoder to see most of the parameter tokens degrades the learned sensor representation in both species. When parameters are restricted strictly to a decoder reconstruction target under matched token budgets, their effect depends on the underlying generator---degrading sorghum representations while improving maize. Even so, supervising parameters alongside visual reconstruction allows our distilled generalist to outperform a parameter-only specialist at parameter regression (achieving a recipe MAE of 0.019 vs.\ 0.036 from point clouds alone), consistent with findings on modality competition~\citep{peng2022ogm} and negative gradient transfer~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}. Furthermore, we show that while adding depth reduces Chamfer distance by 25--28\%, linear probes on frozen representations reveal minimal gain from depth and identify RGB as the primary carrier of morphological trait information. Because Chamfer distance is insensitive to missing thin blades, we adopt frozen-latent probes as our deciding metric~\citep{chen2020simclr,wu2021dcd}.

We validate our approach on crop rows assembled from real maize and sorghum plants with independently reconstructed ground truth~\citep{maizefield3d2025,hadadi2026vlmmaize}. While zero-shot accuracy drops on real plants that exceed the synthetic generator's height range, fine-tuning on 75\% of the real plants brings the held-out test set to a 1.6\% median height error and predicts leaf count within one leaf on 70\% of maize captures, running in milliseconds per plant. Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Task and framework.} We introduce the first amortized framework that recovers complete, editable procedural plant models of slender, row-occluded crops from \emph{any subset} of RGB, depth, and point-cloud inputs in a single forward pass. Full-to-single-sensor distillation reduces generation loss by 30\% in sorghum and 44\% in maize while halving sorghum point-cloud parameter error, and row fine-tuning eliminates 85--92\% of neighbor-induced occlusion error.
  \item \textbf{Design principle for privileged generative modalities.} Through budget-matched ablations across two species, we show that exposing ground-truth procedural parameters to the encoder harms visual representation quality, whereas using them as a decoder-only target helps or hurts depending on the generator's parameter coupling.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation protocol for slender geometry.} We demonstrate that Chamfer distance and downstream trait probes systematically disagree on thin plant organs, and we establish a frozen-latent probing protocol to guide architectural and modality choices where nearest-neighbor geometric metrics fail.
  \item \textbf{The \dataset{} benchmark.} We will release \dataset{}, a 30{,}000-plant, two-species benchmark pairing multimodal sensor captures and physically simulated row occlusion with exact procedural ground truth, alongside a real-field evaluation split across a maize diversity panel~\citep{maizefield3d2025} (Table~\ref{tab:datacompare}).
\end{enumerate}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We briefly review the four areas most closely related to \method{}, with an expanded literature survey provided in Appendix~\ref{app:litreview}.

\noindent\textbf{Recovering procedural plant models.}
Classical inverse procedural modeling estimates tree generator parameters on a per-instance basis using stochastic optimization~\citep{stava2014ipmtrees}, MCMC sampling~\citep{talton2011metropolis}, or L-system induction~\citep{guo2020ipmlsys,latentlsys2023}. More recent crop-focused methods infer parametric morphology from clean, complete 3D point clouds~\citep{demeter2025,ghrer2025paramplants}, optimize procedural parameters against multi-view NeRF reconstructions~\citep{cropcraft2026}, or combine vision-language prompting with differentiable NURBS fitting on terrestrial laser scans~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize,nurbsdiff2022}. However, every existing approach assumes a clean, isolated plant scan or requires minutes of per-plant optimization, leaving missing modalities and dense row-neighbor occlusion unaddressed.

\noindent\textbf{Inferring programs from visual observations.}
Outside of agriculture, feed-forward models have been trained to infer 3D shape programs~\citep{shapeprograms2019}, diffusion-based procedural assets~\citep{zhao2025dipcg}, and executable CAD command sequences~\citep{text2cad2024,kolodiazhnyi2025cadrille} from images or point clouds. These systems target rigid, isolated man-made objects under clean viewing conditions and do not examine whether continuous program parameters should be fed into the encoder during multimodal pretraining.

\noindent\textbf{Point-cloud completion and slender geometry.}
Deep point-cloud completion networks~\citep{yuan2018pcn,yu2021pointr,yu2023adapointr,cai2024einet} are primarily developed on compact, mostly convex shapes. Recent work on occluded plant completion~\citep{lee2026occ} and thin-structure reconstruction~\citep{curvegaussian2025,qi2026rethinking} handles slender geometry, but outputs unstructured point sets rather than editable, trait-level procedural models. Moreover, standard Chamfer distance is structurally biased on thin surfaces because clustering points along thick stems yields low nearest-neighbor error even when entire leaves are missing~\citep{wu2021dcd,song2026structural,lin2023infocd}. We address this by pairing distribution-matching losses with frozen-latent trait probes (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}).

\noindent\textbf{Multimodal masked pretraining and synthetic data.}
Masked autoencoding~\citep{he2022mae} has been extended to multimodal images~\citep{bachmann2022multimae}, any-to-any generation~\citep{mizrahi2023fourm,bachmann2024fourm21}, 3D point clouds~\citep{pang2022pointmae}, and joint RGB--depth--point-cloud inputs in EmbodiedMAE~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}, which serves as our architectural backbone. We introduce a fourth structured modality---continuous procedural parameters embedded via xVal-style numerical tokens~\citep{golkar2023xval}---and distill a full-modality teacher~\citep{hinton2015distillation} into single-sensor students to handle missing modalities at test time~\citep{wang2024robustmissing}. While pretraining on procedural synthetic data is well established~\citep{kataoka2020fractaldb,raistrick2023infinigen,greff2022kubric,tobin2017domainrand,fan2023intriguing}, our setting is uniquely aligned because the generator defines the exact target output space and is validated directly against real-field scans~\citep{maizefield3d2025}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We first formalize inverse procedural plant reconstruction (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-form}) and describe our \dataset{} synthetic data engine (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-data}). We then detail our four-stream masked pretraining (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-pretrain}), cross-modal teacher--student distillation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-distill}), and field evaluation protocols.

\subsection{Problem Formulation: Inverse Procedural Reconstruction}
\label{sec:method-form}

Let $G$ denote a species-specific procedural plant generator that maps a compact parameter vector $\theta \in \R^{d_\theta}$ to an editable 3D mesh $G(\theta)$. The vector $\theta$ provides a complete structural recipe for the plant, specifying global traits such as stem height and internode spacing alongside per-leaf attributes including insertion height, blade length and width, and insertion, roll, and azimuth angles.

In practical field campaigns, rover-mounted sensors produce partial, noisy, and occluded observations from a subset of modalities $S \subseteq \mathcal{M} = \{\mathrm{rgb}, \mathrm{d}, \mathrm{pc}\}$, where $\mathrm{rgb}$ denotes a color image, $\mathrm{d}$ a metric depth map, and $\mathrm{pc}$ a 3D point cloud. Our goal is to learn an amortized feed-forward inverse mapping,
\begin{equation}
  f_\phi\!\left(\{x^m : m \in S\}\right)
  = \left(\hat\theta, \{\hat x^m : m \in \mathcal{M}\}\right),
  \label{eq:procedural-inverse}
\end{equation}
that predicts both the complete procedural parameter vector $\hat\theta$ and the completed sensor streams $\{\hat x^m\}$ in a single forward pass. Unlike unstructured point-cloud completion, which must extrapolate hidden surfaces point by point, predicting $\hat\theta$ leverages $G$ as an explicit biological prior: re-rendering $G(\hat\theta)$ produces watertight, structurally valid, and editable 3D geometry in milliseconds rather than minutes or hours of per-plant optimization.

\noindent\textbf{Data generation overview.}
Using species-specific procedural generators~\citep{vazquez_phenoframe_2026,ostermann_sorghum_2024}, we synthesize 15{,}000 plants each for maize and sorghum. Maize plants span 0.62--1.44\,m in height with 3--22 leaves governed by four coupled latent traits, leaving height and leaf count only weakly correlated ($r = 0.20$). In contrast, sorghum plants (generated via EvoEngine) have stem lengths of 0.19--1.74\,m and 4--35 leaves tightly coupled to stem length ($R^2 = 0.988$). Together, the two species test \method{} across both weakly and strongly coupled morphological regimes.

For isolated-plant pretraining, we render ten $1024 \times 1024$ views per plant ($40^\circ$ field of view) with RGB imagery, losslessly packed metric depth, and camera calibration. Sorghum is captured from a fixed 10-camera Fibonacci rig at 2.5\,m with complete 8{,}192-point plant clouds per view, whereas maize is captured from random spherical directions at bounding-box-fitted distances with partial 8{,}192-point clouds back-projected directly from the depth map. To simulate field rows, each target plant is placed between two neighbors spaced 0.15\,m apart across ten row orientations ($0^\circ$--$90^\circ$ yaw, with $\pm 15^\circ$ random neighbor azimuth jitter). An articulated contact solver models leaf blades as chains of 8\,cm jointed segments and resolves all triangle--triangle collisions so neighbors yield first while the target plant retains its grown shape wherever possible. A simulated ground-rover camera captures the crowded row (RGB, depth, and a 24{,}576-point scene cloud) paired with the contact-bent target plant alone (8{,}192 points) as ground truth. In these rows, neighbors account for 59--68\% of all plant pixels in maize and occlude 23\% of the target plant's pixels in sorghum. We split plants 70\% / 15\% / 15\% into train, validation, and test sets, enriching the validation and test splits toward extreme morphologies via Mahalanobis ranking. Full generation, rendering, and contact-solver details appear in Appendix~\ref{app:data}.

\subsection{Four-Stream Masked Pretraining}
\label{sec:method-pretrain}

\noindent\textbf{Multimodal tokenization.}
Extending EmbodiedMAE~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}, we tokenize all four plant modalities into a unified sequence. RGB and depth images are partitioned into $14 \times 14 = 196$ patches and linearly projected to 768-dimensional embeddings with 2D sine--cosine positional encodings. Point clouds are grouped into 196 neighborhoods of 32 points via farthest-point sampling and encoded with a lightweight PointNet~\citep{pang2022pointmae,qi2017pointnet}. The procedural parameter vector $\theta$ is structured into $1 + K$ continuous numerical tokens---one global plant token followed by $K$ ordered leaf slots ($K = 28$ for maize, $K = 24$ for sorghum)---affinely normalized to $[0,1]$ and embedded using xVal-style continuous value scaling~\citep{golkar2023xval}. After adding learned modality embeddings and a global \texttt{[CLS]} token, visible tokens are processed by a 12-block ViT encoder (width 768) and reconstructed by an 8-block decoder (width 512) with linear heads for RGB and depth, a FoldingNet head (41 points per token) for point clouds, and an MLP head for procedural parameters. During training, valid leaf slots are selected via a ground-truth existence mask; at test time without ground truth (Table~\ref{tab:regime1}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), leaf count is inferred directly from the predicted recipe (explicitly stored in the plant token for maize, and derived from predicted stem length for sorghum).

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Dirichlet masking.}
To train a single network capable of handling any subset of sensor inputs, we mask a fraction $\rho = 0.80$ of all tokens at each step, leaving $V = \lfloor (1-\rho)L \rfloor$ visible tokens ($122$ of $L = 613$ tokens in sorghum; $123$ of $617$ in maize). Visible tokens are allocated across the four streams using a symmetric Dirichlet draw at every iteration, constrained so that each active stream retains at least one visible token and at least 25\% masked tokens. In our default configuration, the parameter stream shares this visibility budget ($\sim 58\%$ visible on average), whereas our controlled ablations in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params} gate parameter visibility at a fixed rate $\rho_\theta$ (with $\rho_\theta = 1$ making parameters a pure decoder reconstruction target).

\noindent\textbf{Multi-term reconstruction objective.}
We train the network with a weighted sum of modality-specific reconstruction losses:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} = \ell_{\mathrm{rgb}} + \ell_{\mathrm{d}} + \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} + 5\ell_\theta,
  \label{eq:reconstruction-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{\mathrm{rgb}}$ is the MSE on patch-normalized RGB pixels~\citep{he2022mae}, $\ell_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the MSE on min--max normalized depth, and $\ell_\theta$ is the Smooth-$L_1$ loss on masked parameter tokens. Because standard Chamfer distance allows decoders to minimize error by clustering points onto thick stems while omitting thin leaf blades~\citep{wu2021dcd,song2026structural}, we use a sigmoid-weighted quality-aware loss (QAL) that focuses gradients on poorly reconstructed regions:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} = \frac{1}{|\hat P|}\sum_{p\in\hat P}w(\delta(p,P))\,\delta(p,P) + \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{q\in P}w(\delta(q,\hat P))\,\delta(q,\hat P),
  \label{eq:point-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(p,P) = \min_{q \in P} \|p - q\|_2$ and $w(r) = \sigma(a(r - \tau))$ with sharpness $a = 100$ and tolerance $\tau = 0.01$ in unit-sphere coordinates. For our headline models, we further add a debiased Sinkhorn optimal transport divergence~\citep{feydy2019sinkhorn} over 1{,}024-point subsets ($\varepsilon = 0.02$) to encourage global coverage of thin foliage:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\mathrm{pc}}^{\star} = \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} + 0.5\,S_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\hat P_{1024}, P_{1024}\right).
  \label{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}
\end{equation}
All models are trained with AdamW and cosine learning-rate decay. Our headline models train for 1{,}000 epochs over all ten views (global batch size 256, peak learning rate $6 \times 10^{-4}$), whereas controlled ablations share a fixed compute budget of 197{,}400 steps (global batch size 32, one random view per plant per epoch, peak learning rate $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$) using Eq.~\eqref{eq:point-loss}.

\subsection{Full-Modality Teacher to Single-Sensor Students}
\label{sec:distill}\label{sec:method-distill}

At deployment, a single-sensor model sees only one modality while every token of the remaining streams is absent---a configuration rarely encountered under random Dirichlet masking. To bridge this gap, we distill a frozen four-stream teacher into single-sensor student models. The teacher receives privileged access to the procedural parameters (all parameter tokens visible for sorghum; 50\% of each stream visible for maize, where full visibility degrades cloud reconstruction) and produces a global latent vector $c^T \in \R^{768}$ and normalized decoder features $F^T \in \R^{L \times 512}$.

Each student is initialized from the teacher's weights and trained for 100 epochs (AdamW, learning rate $10^{-4}$, global batch size 128). On 80\% of training steps, the student receives only a single active source stream $s \in \{\mathrm{rgb}, \mathrm{d}, \mathrm{pc}, \theta\}$ (with all other streams completely masked); on the remaining 20\%, it follows standard four-stream masked pretraining. The student minimizes:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{student}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + \frac{\lambda_F}{|\mathcal{J}_{\neg s}|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\neg s}} \|F^S_j - F^T_j\|_2^2 + \lambda_c \|c^S - c^T\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:distillation-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{J}_{\neg s}$ indexes the decoder positions of the unobserved streams, $\lambda_F = 1.0$, and $\lambda_c = 0.5$. At inference, each student operates solely on its designated input sensor.

\noindent\textbf{Field evaluation protocols.}
We evaluate \method{} across three complementary protocols:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Trait probing:} We fit ridge regression probes on frozen \texttt{[CLS]} representations using the training split and evaluate $R^2$ on held-out plants (using one deterministic view per plant with parameter inputs zeroed out to prevent label leakage). A difference is counted as significant only when its 95\% paired-bootstrap confidence interval (2{,}000 plant resamples) excludes zero on both validation and test splits. Under partial point-cloud visibility, probes are either refit per visibility level (\emph{matched}) or transferred directly from complete clouds (\emph{transferred}).
  \item \textbf{Row occlusion:} We test both zero-shot transfer of isolated-trained models and row fine-tuned models on simulated crop rows, evaluating reconstruction accuracy strictly against the occluded target plant and verifying via an echo test that models do not merely copy neighboring foliage.
  \item \textbf{Real-field validation:} We evaluate row models on crop rows assembled from real-world MaizeField3D captures~\citep{maizefield3d2025}, benchmarking against procedural fits produced by a staged vision-language pipeline~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Regime I: Single-View Reconstruction of Isolated Plants}
\label{sec:regime1}\label{sec:exp-regime1}

Table~\ref{tab:regime1} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) evaluates single-sensor inference on isolated validation plants, where the model receives a single RGB image, depth map, or point cloud and outputs both the procedural parameters and the remaining sensor streams in one forward pass. Across all single-sensor inputs, \method{} recovers the procedural plant reliably: predicted leaf count falls within one leaf of ground truth on 91--100\% of plants, height error is 2--5\%, and mean leaf-parameter skill reaches 0.66--0.68 in sorghum and 0.57--0.59 in maize. Skill is highest for leaf insertion height and angle, moderate for blade length, and lowest for sorghum leaf width, which exhibits narrow variation across plants. Averaging predicted recipes across three views improves accuracy across every metric. Conversely, a two-stage baseline that first predicts a point cloud from RGB and then infers parameters from the generated cloud loses almost all parameter accuracy (sorghum leaf skill drops from 0.66 to $-0.01$), confirming the benefit of direct end-to-end parameter prediction.

The reconstructed 3D geometry mirrors these trends. Depth is the strongest single input for point-cloud generation (Chamfer $0.93 \times 10^{-3}$ in sorghum and $0.64 \times 10^{-3}$ in maize, corresponding to median RMS errors of 30\,mm and 12\,mm, and $F_1$ scores of 0.44 and 0.53), with RGB within a factor of 1.5 and achieving a test-split skill of 0.98--0.99 relative to random plant pairs (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-distill}). Notably, giving the undistilled headline model all three streams performs no better than giving the distilled generalist a single sensor, since the undistilled model was trained with 80\% masking rather than full-stream visibility. Qualitatively, Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} (Appendix~\ref{app:figures}) shows that the distilled generalist resolves crisp, individual leaf blades from a single photograph where the undistilled model produces diffuse point clouds.

\subsection{Regime II: Viewpoint Elevation, Self-Occlusion, and Complexity}
\label{sec:regime2}\label{sec:exp-regime2}

\noindent\textbf{Effect of camera elevation.}
Because all ten views of a plant share the exact same ground-truth procedural parameters, evaluating across the elevation ladder isolates the effect of camera angle. Single-image reconstruction error follows a symmetric U-shaped curve across elevation, lowest from horizontal side views and highest when looking steeply down or up (Fig.~\ref{fig:views}A). Camera elevation accounts for $\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$ of per-plant variance, whereas morphological traits correlate negligibly ($|\rho| \le 0.06$). Distillation reduces error by 28\% across all viewpoints while preserving the underlying U-shaped difficulty profile.

\noindent\textbf{Training view distribution.}
Models pretrained on narrow elevation bands (top-down views $i \in \{0,1\}$, horizontal views $i \in \{4,5\}$, or a single top-down view $i=0$) excel only at their trained angles and degrade sharply elsewhere: sorghum per-view probe $R^2$ drops by 0.066--0.144 on unseen elevations, whereas training across all ten views maintains $R^2 = 0.734\text{--}0.778$ everywhere (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}). In maize, every restricted-view model loses on 10--11 of 11 traits under paired bootstraps, with point-cloud $F_1$ showing the same pattern. Covering the full range of evaluation elevations thus matters far more than image count alone (Appendix~\ref{app:views-full}).

\noindent\textbf{Self-occlusion under partial coverage.}
When point clouds are restricted via mesh ray-casting to surfaces visible from 1 or 3 cameras (49\% and 78\% surface coverage), latent trait information remains intact if the linear probe is refit to the partial distribution (achieving 0.960 height $R^2$ from a single camera for the headline model). However, applying a probe trained on 100\% complete clouds fails completely ($R^2 \to -0.26$; Fig.~\ref{fig:partial}, Table~\ref{tab:views}). Partial visibility therefore induces a systematic covariate shift in the latent space, meaning field readouts must be calibrated on sensor-matched coverage.

\noindent\textbf{Morphological complexity.}
Following distillation, single-image reconstruction error remains invariant across leaf-count deciles (Spearman $\rho = -0.07$ in sorghum and $+0.04$ in maize; Fig.~\ref{fig:leafcount}), showing that \method{} scales gracefully to complex canopies.

\subsection{Regime III: Neighbor Occlusion in Realistic Crop Rows}
\label{sec:regime3}\label{sec:exp-occlusion}

Table~\ref{tab:regime_3} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) compares reconstruction accuracy on identical test plants evaluated in isolation versus inside densely planted crop rows. Models trained solely on isolated plants break down under row occlusion, with Chamfer error jumping sixteenfold in maize ($1.8 \to 29.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^2$) and tenfold in sorghum ($13.1 \to 133.8 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^2$). Our echo test confirms that these models mistakenly reconstruct neighboring foliage as part of the target plant. Fine-tuning on simulated rows eliminates 92\% of the neighbor-induced error in maize ($29.0 \to 4.0$) and 85\% in sorghum ($133.8 \to 30.7$), cleanly isolating the target plant behind its neighbors. Procedural parameter accuracy improves in tandem: row fine-tuning reduces leaf parameter MAE on occluded rows from 0.093 to 0.052 in maize and from 0.096 to 0.061 in sorghum (Fig.~\ref{fig:rowsex}). In maize, whose leaves align roughly parallel to the row, error peaks in broadside views and drops in end-on views, whereas sorghum error is invariant to row yaw due to its random leaf azimuths (Fig.~\ref{fig:row_reconstruction_quant}).

\subsection{Any-Subset Modality Reconstruction and Distillation}
\label{sec:res-distill}\label{sec:exp-main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) summarizes cross-modal generation performance before and after teacher--student distillation, illustrated qualitatively in Figs.~\ref{fig:any2any-maize} and~\ref{fig:any2any-sorghum}. Distillation reduces overall cross-modal generation loss by 30.2\% in sorghum and 43.5\% in maize. Gains are largest on the hardest single-sensor conversions: in sorghum, RGB-to-parameter MAE drops by 76\% ($0.058 \to 0.014$), point-cloud-to-parameter MAE drops in half ($0.040 \to 0.020$), and RGB-to-cloud Chamfer improves by 61\% ($2.27 \to 0.88 \times 10^{-3}$). Combining a visual sensor with procedural parameters yields the lowest geometric errors ($0.36\text{--}0.44 \times 10^{-3}$), and a single unified generalist student trained across all input pairs performs within $0.03$ of dedicated two-stream specialists in both species.

\subsection{Influence of Procedural Parameter Stream Placement}
\label{sec:param-stream}\label{sec:exp-params}

Tables~\ref{tab:modality_value_add} and~\ref{tab:token_budget} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) examine how active input modalities and parameter-stream visibility shape the learned encoder representation. Visual streams provide the primary morphological signal: adding RGB or depth to point clouds lifts sorghum height $R^2$ from 0.895 to 0.979 and 0.976, while adding RGB lifts maize height $R^2$ from 0.171 to 0.620. By contrast, although depth improves Chamfer distance by 25--28\%, it yields almost no gain in frozen-probe $R^2$.

Exposing procedural parameter tokens to the encoder degrades sensor representations in both species. To separate token-budget dilution from shortcut learning, Table~\ref{tab:token_budget} holds the visible sensor token budget fixed at 117.0 tokens while varying parameter visibility. In sorghum, adding the parameter stream degrades every probed trait regardless of visibility level---even when used strictly as a decoder-only reconstruction target ($R^2$ drops from 0.977 to 0.966 on height). In maize, however, gating parameter visibility to 20\% or using parameters purely as a decoder target improves all eleven traits (lifting height $R^2$ from 0.633 to 0.776). We attribute this divergence to shortcut learning when parameters are visible to the encoder, combined with the structural rank of each generator's parameter space: sorghum parameters are tightly coupled (effective rank two), pulling decoder gradients toward global size, whereas maize spans eleven independent morphological axes that act as rich multi-task supervision (Figs.~\ref{fig:stream_contributions} and~\ref{fig:controls}).

\subsection{Divergence Between Chamfer Distance and Probing Metrics}
\label{sec:res-metrics}\label{sec:exp-metrics}

Across twelve architectural variants, Chamfer distance and frozen-probe $R^2$ correlate broadly (Spearman $\rho = -0.57$ to $-0.88$) but disagree on critical design choices (Fig.~\ref{fig:metric_divergence}). Adding depth cuts Chamfer by 25--28\% without improving trait probing, and adding parameter tokens improves maize Chamfer while degrading sorghum representations. Because crop leaves are thin, high-aspect-ratio sheets, clustering predicted points along the central stem yields low nearest-neighbor distance even when whole blades are missing: precision exceeds recall across every model variant (Fig.~\ref{fig:precision_recall}). Our loss ablation (Appendix~\ref{sec:abl_loss}) confirms this mechanism: QAL alone places points precisely on stems but achieves low recall, Sinkhorn divergence alone maximizes coverage with poor precision, and combining both (Eq.~\ref{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}) yields the best $F_1$ across thresholds. We therefore rely on frozen-latent probes to guide modality decisions while training with the combined QAL + Sinkhorn loss.

\subsection{Real-World Field Validation}
\label{sec:res-field}\label{sec:exp-real}

We next evaluate our row models on crop rows constructed from real-world plants: procedural reconstructions of 520 real maize plants from MaizeField3D~\citep{maizefield3d2025,hadadi2026vlmmaize} and 30 laser-scanned real sorghum plants (2.2--5.3\,m tall). Plants are arranged into rows at 15--25\,cm spacing in two orientations (leaf planes parallel and perpendicular to the row) and imaged across 18 rover camera stops per row (9{,}360 stops per layout for maize; 540 for sorghum), retaining synthetic rendering to isolate morphological sim-to-real transfer from sensor noise.

Evaluated zero-shot, the row fine-tuned model outperforms both the undistilled headline model and the isolated control across both maize row layouts (Table~\ref{tab:e10}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}), confirming that row-occlusion reasoning transfers to real plant geometry. However, absolute errors remain roughly an order of magnitude higher than on synthetic rows due to a size mismatch: 83\% of the real maize plants are taller than the tallest synthetic training plant (1.33\,m), capping zero-shot predictions around 1.24\,m (median height error is 14\% on in-range plants vs.\ 38\% on taller plants). Similarly, real sorghum plants (up to 5.3\,m) far exceed the synthetic training range (1.74\,m), preventing zero-shot transfer.

Fine-tuning the row model for 15 epochs on 75\% of the real plants (split strictly by plant) closes most of this gap on the held-out 25\% (130 maize plants and 8 sorghum plants): median maize height error drops from 36\% to 1.6\%, leaf count accuracy within $\pm 1$ rises from 45\% to 70\%, maize Chamfer falls from 59.5 to $19.7 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^2$, and sorghum Chamfer drops from 2{,}113 to $79.4 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}^2$. These improvements hold consistently across single- and multi-sensor inputs, row layouts, and occlusion levels (Table~\ref{tab:e10_detail}; Figs.~\ref{fig:e10_real}, \ref{fig:e10_real_sorghum}, and~\ref{fig:e10_height}), requiring only 10.6\,ms per camera stop at inference.

\subsection{Representation Quality, Scaling, and Transfer}
\label{sec:res-repr}\label{sec:exp-repr}

\noindent\textbf{Comparison against published encoders.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} benchmarks \method{} against DINOv2, MultiMAE, Point-MAE, and EmbodiedMAE using identical mean-pooled token readouts and only the input modalities each encoder supports (Appendix~\ref{app:probe}). Three-stream \method{} ($\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}$) outperforms all baselines on 8 of 9 sorghum traits and 10 of 11 maize traits, while our 1{,}000-epoch headline model achieves the strongest overall representation in the table, beating every published encoder on all eleven maize traits. Notably, full-pose Point-MAE appears artificially strong on synthetic maize due to a renderer artifact in which canonical leaf alignments leak azimuth through camera pose matrices; under realistic gravity-aligned poses, Point-MAE height $R^2$ drops from 0.720 to 0.588, whereas pose-free \method{} is unaffected (Appendix~\ref{app:probe}). Masked reconstruction comparisons are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-recon}.

\noindent\textbf{Scaling, cross-species transfer, and latent geometry.}
Increasing training dataset size from 1{,}000 to 10{,}500 plants monotonically reduces Chamfer distance ($3.13 \to 2.53 \times 10^{-3}$ in sorghum; $3.54 \to 0.99 \times 10^{-3}$ in maize), whereas maize probe scores fluctuate at intermediate scales before peaking at the full dataset. Under a fixed gradient-step budget, scaling backbone capacity from ViT-Base (114\,M) to ViT-Large (332\,M) degrades representation quality in both species (Table~\ref{tab:scaling_ablations}, Fig.~\ref{fig:data_model_scaling_fig}). Pretraining on maize and fine-tuning for 150 epochs on sorghum outperforms training from scratch across every probed trait (validation Chamfer $1.83$ vs.\ $3.38 \times 10^{-3}$), although decoders do not transfer zero-shot across species. Finally, t-SNE projections of frozen \texttt{[CLS]} tokens reveal smooth, continuous manifolds organized by plant scale and leaf posture rather than discrete clusters (Fig.~\ref{fig:latent_tsne}), with low effective participation ratios (4.4--6.3 of 768 dimensions in sorghum; 6.7--11.3 in maize).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% 5. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We summarize the main takeaways and limitations of our study, with a fuller discussion in Appendix~\ref{app:discussion}.

\noindent\textbf{Design rule for privileged generative parameters.}
Because procedural parameter tokens state the target morphological traits directly yet are unavailable at field deployment, allowing the encoder to observe them creates a shortcut that starves visual and point-cloud encoders of gradient signal. This mirrors modality competition~\citep{peng2022ogm} and negative gradient transfer~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}. Our budget-matched controls (Table~\ref{tab:token_budget}) demonstrate that privileged generative parameters should never be largely visible to the encoder during pretraining; when restricted strictly to a decoder target, their benefit depends on whether the generator's parameter space spans independent traits (helping maize) or collapses onto a low-rank axis (hurting sorghum).

\noindent\textbf{Evaluating slender 3D geometry.}
Nearest-neighbor metrics such as Chamfer distance reward points clustered along thick central stems while overlooking missing leaf blades. Because Chamfer distance and frozen-latent trait probes diverge on depth inclusion, dataset scaling, and partial visibility (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}), geometric metrics alone are insufficient for thin botanical structures. Pairing distribution-matching training objectives (Eq.~\ref{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}) with downstream trait probes provides a much more reliable signal.

\noindent\textbf{Partial sensing and row occlusion.}
Camera elevation is the primary driver of single-view difficulty ($\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$). Under partial point-cloud coverage, morphological information remains preserved in the latent space but undergoes a covariate shift, requiring linear readouts to be refit on sensor-matched inputs. In dense rows, training with physically simulated contact occlusion enables the network to separate the target plant from interleaved neighbors, eliminating 85--92\% of neighbor-induced error.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.}
First, controlled ablations were run with a single random seed per arm under a fixed step budget (run-to-run variation is roughly 2\% in Chamfer and $\le 0.011$ in probe $R^2$). Second, inverse procedural reconstruction is inherently bounded by the expressivity of the underlying generator $G$: morphological anomalies absent from $G$---such as severe disease lesions, broken stalks, or unmodeled tassel droop---are projected onto the nearest valid procedural configuration. Third, synthetic rendering pipelines can introduce subtle pose regularities, underscoring the importance of real-world validation (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-real}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% 6. CONCLUSION
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\method{} shows that sparse, heavily occluded field observations are sufficient to recover complete, editable procedural plant models when the procedural generator serves as both the training data engine and the structured output space. By combining four-stream masked pretraining, cross-modal teacher--student distillation, and physically simulated row-occlusion fine-tuning, our framework converts any subset of RGB, depth, and point-cloud inputs into biologically interpretable crop models in milliseconds. Distillation cuts cross-modal generation loss by 30--44\%, row fine-tuning eliminates 85--92\% of neighbor-induced occlusion error, and brief fine-tuning on real plants brings held-out maize height error down to 1.6\%. Beyond digital agriculture, our experiments establish two general principles for structured 3D learning: privileged generative parameters should be withheld from the encoder during multimodal pretraining, and downstream trait probes should accompany nearest-neighbor geometric metrics whenever evaluating slender, occluded objects. We will release \dataset{}---comprising 30{,}000 synthetic plants across two species with multimodal captures, simulated row occlusion, exact procedural ground truth, and a real-field evaluation split---to support further research in 3D plant phenotyping and inverse procedural modeling.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{app:metrics}

Geometric accuracy is measured by symmetric squared Chamfer distance in the unit-sphere normalized frame ($\times 10^{-3}$; a value of $1.0$ corresponds to roughly 33\,mm RMS error on a median sorghum plant and 19\,mm on a median maize plant, computed using per-plant metric scale factors). Pretrained models are evaluated under their 80\% masked pretraining regime, whereas distilled students are evaluated with unmasked inputs.

Procedural parameter accuracy is measured as mean absolute error (MAE) across normalized $[0,1]$ fields of masked, non-padding leaf tokens, and is also expressed as normalized trait \emph{skill}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Skill} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{MAE}}{\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{mean}}},
  \label{eq:trait-skill}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{mean}}$ is the error of predicting the training-set mean, so $\mathrm{Skill} = 1$ denotes perfect recovery and $\mathrm{Skill} = 0$ corresponds to predicting the dataset mean. Representation quality is evaluated via ridge regression $R^2$ on frozen encoder features with 95\% paired-bootstrap confidence intervals across 2{,}250 validation and 2{,}250 test plants. Because sorghum's four primary developmental traits have effective rank two (stem length and leaf count correlate at $r = 0.994$), sorghum probes also include independent generator axes (mean leaf roll and blade length), whereas maize spans eleven largely independent morphological traits.

% APPENDIX D: ENCODER-COMPARISON PROBE PROTOCOL

\section{Encoder-Comparison Probe Protocol}
\label{app:probe}

\noindent\textbf{Standardized probing protocol.}
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} evaluates all encoders under an identical linear probing protocol adapted so every baseline can participate fairly. First, each plant is represented by mean-pooling its spatial/point output tokens (excluding the \texttt{[CLS]} token in \method{}), which every encoder supports. Second, each model receives only the input modalities it was pretrained on from view 00: DINOv2 receives RGB, MultiMAE receives RGB and depth, Point-MAE receives the point cloud, EmbodiedMAE receives all three sensor streams, and \method{} receives its pretrained sensor streams (procedural parameter inputs are always excluded). For every encoder and trait, token features across the 10{,}500 training plants are standardized using training-split statistics alone, and a ridge regressor is fit with regularization strength $\alpha \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 10^2, 10^3, 10^4\}$ chosen via five-fold cross-validation. We report $R^2$ on the 2{,}250 validation plants; an end-to-end supervised baseline trained directly on trait labels is probed in the exact same way on its frozen penultimate features. Probed ground-truth traits are stem length, leaf count, mean branching angle, mean leaf roll, and mean leaf length in sorghum, and summed internode length, mean leaf angle, mean leaf curvature, and stem radius in maize. Because validation and test splits are enriched with extreme phenotypes ($1.8\times$ training variance in sorghum and $1.5\text{--}2.6\times$ in maize), these $R^2$ values provide a strict internal ranking across encoders rather than absolute scores comparable to unenriched datasets. Test $R^2$ closely tracks validation (e.g., maize headline height reaches 0.747 on validation and 0.759 on test).

\noindent\textbf{Camera pose artifact analysis.}
When Point-MAE is supplied with full 3D camera pose matrices on synthetic maize, its apparent accuracy is inflated by a rendering regularity: synthetic maize models align leaf planes along a canonical world axis, allowing full pose matrices to leak plant azimuth. Evaluating Point-MAE with realistic gravity-aligned camera poses (where azimuth is uncalibrated) reduces its height $R^2$ from 0.720 to 0.588, whereas pose-free \method{} is unaffected.

\section{Extended Discussion}
\label{app:discussion}

We expand below on the architectural, methodological, and practical findings from our experiments.

\noindent\textbf{Privileged parameter visibility and modality competition.}
A central question when pretraining multimodal models alongside procedural generators is how ground-truth structural parameters should be integrated. The parameter stream $\theta$ states the target phenotypic traits explicitly, yet it is never observed at deployment. Across our ablations (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params}), allowing the encoder to attend to parameter tokens consistently degraded sensor representation quality in both species (Table~\ref{tab:token_budget}).

This behavior aligns with shortcut learning, modality competition~\citep{peng2022ogm}, and negative gradient transfer~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}. Because parameter tokens directly encode the target geometry, the encoder learns a low-cost shortcut through them, starving the RGB, depth, and point-cloud encoders of informative gradient updates. When parameter tokens are removed at inference, the resulting sensor representations underperform.

Our budget-matched controls (Table~\ref{tab:token_budget}) further reveal how decoder-only parameter supervision interacts with generator structure:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sorghum:} Keeping parameters strictly as a decoder target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) still reduces sensor probe $R^2$ relative to a three-stream model (height drops from 0.977 to 0.966). Because sorghum parameters have an effective rank of two, decoder gradients pull the shared latent space toward global plant scale (PC1 accounts for 44\% of latent variance and correlates with leaf count at $|r| = 0.95$, reducing participation ratio from 6.7 to 4.3).
  \item \textbf{Maize:} Keeping parameters as a decoder target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) or gating them to 20\% visibility improves probe $R^2$ across all eleven traits (lifting height $R^2$ from 0.633 to 0.776). Because maize spans eleven independent morphological axes, reconstructing parameters provides rich, multi-task structural supervision without inducing encoder shortcuts.
\end{itemize}
Together, these results yield a clear design rule: \emph{privileged generative parameters should never be largely visible to the encoder during pretraining}. Restricting them to a decoder reconstruction target (or heavy gating $\rho_\theta \le 0.20$) eliminates encoder shortcuts while retaining generative parameter prediction.

\noindent\textbf{Metric divergence on slender geometry.}
Standard Chamfer distance measures average nearest-neighbor surface displacement:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CD}(P, \hat P) = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\min_{q\in\hat P}\|p-q\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{|\hat P|}\sum_{q\in\hat P}\min_{p\in P}\|p-q\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:chamfer_def}
\end{equation}
On thin, high-aspect-ratio leaves, a network can achieve low Chamfer error simply by concentrating points along the thicker central stem while omitting entire leaf blades. Frozen-latent trait probes directly test whether the encoder captures full morphological structure. Consequently, the two metrics disagree on key design decisions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Adding depth:} Depth cuts Chamfer distance by 28\% in sorghum and 25\% in maize, yet leaves frozen-probe $R^2$ virtually unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:modality_value_add}), indicating that depth refines local surface placement in the decoder without enriching high-level structural features in the encoder.
  \item \textbf{Dataset scaling:} Scaling from 1{,}000 to 10{,}000 sorghum plants lowers Chamfer by 21\% ($3.13 \to 2.48 \times 10^{-3}$), whereas probe scores saturate (Table~\ref{tab:scaling_ablations}).
  \item \textbf{Partial visibility:} Single-camera clouds increase Chamfer error by $2.6\times$ to $6.5\times$, while refit probes on fused multimodal inputs lose at most 0.030 in height $R^2$ (Table~\ref{tab:views}).
\end{enumerate}
Combining distribution-matching losses (Eq.~\ref{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}) with downstream trait probes is therefore essential when developing models for thin plant geometry.

\noindent\textbf{Partial sensing, covariate shift, and row occlusion.}
Field observations are inherently incomplete, and our experiments clarify three failure modes and remedies: (1) camera elevation accounts for $\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$ of single-view error variance because steep top-down or bottom-up views cause leaves to self-occlude along the optical axis; (2) partial cloud coverage preserves latent trait information but shifts feature statistics, causing probes trained on complete clouds to fail ($R^2 \to -0.26$) unless refit on sensor-matched inputs; and (3) fine-tuning on simulated crop rows trains the network to segment out interleaved neighbor foliage, removing 92\% (maize) and 85\% (sorghum) of neighbor-induced error. Moreover, because $G(\hat\theta)$ enforces a valid botanical structure, procedural outputs degrade far more gracefully under occlusion than unstructured point clouds.

\noindent\textbf{Capabilities and limits of cross-modal distillation.}
Distilling a four-stream teacher into single-sensor students lowers cross-modal generation loss by 30.2\% in sorghum and 43.5\% in maize (Table~\ref{tab:anysubset}), concentrating gains on weak input modalities (e.g., cutting RGB-to-cloud Chamfer by 61\%) and difficult plant geometries (Fig.~\ref{fig:views}B). Jointly supervising all four output streams also outperforms a parameter-only specialist student (point-cloud recipe MAE of 0.019 vs.\ 0.036 and 0.038 at learning rates $10^{-4}$ and $3 \times 10^{-5}$ from the same warm start at 0.040), showing that auxiliary visual reconstruction regularizes parameter regression. However, distillation cannot compensate for missing camera viewpoints during pretraining (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}), and parameters submitted to inverse regression must be physically observable from field sensors (early experiments with unobservable leaf waviness parameters exhibited high view-dependent variance and near-zero skill, prompting their removal from the final generator).

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and future work.}
First, controlled ablations were run with one seed per configuration at a fixed 197{,}400-step budget (estimated run-to-run variation is $\approx 2\%$ in Chamfer and $\le 0.011$ in probe $R^2$). Second, recovered models are constrained by the expressivity of the procedural generator $G$; field anomalies such as severe disease, broken stems, or unmodeled tassel droop cannot be represented exactly and are mapped to the closest valid procedural plant. Expanding procedural grammars to capture stress and damage phenotypes is an important next step. Third, real-field evaluation remains vital to guard against synthetic renderer cues.

\section{Detailed Literature Review}
\label{app:litreview}

This appendix expands upon Section~\ref{sec:related} across six related areas.

\subsection{Procedural Plant Models and Inverse Procedural Modeling}
Procedural plant modeling began with Lindenmayer systems (L-systems)~\citep{prusin1990abop} and has evolved into parametric crop generators whose parameters correspond directly to agronomic traits~\citep{floraforge2026,hadadi2025procmaize}. Early inverse procedural modeling recovered tree generator parameters per instance using stochastic search~\citep{stava2014ipmtrees}, MCMC inference~\citep{talton2011metropolis}, and L-system induction~\citep{guo2020ipmlsys,latentlsys2023}. Feed-forward inverse approaches have recently emerged for CAD and man-made shapes---including shape programs~\citep{shapeprograms2019}, diffusion-based inverse procedural generation~\citep{zhao2025dipcg}, Text2CAD~\citep{text2cad2024}, and cadrille~\citep{kolodiazhnyi2025cadrille}---but they operate on rigid, isolated objects without inter-object occlusion or deformable thin organs.

\subsection{Learned Parametric Recovery of Crop Plants}
Recent agricultural vision systems infer parametric crop representations from 3D observations. Demeter~\citep{demeter2025} learns articulated soybean morphology from point clouds, while Ghrer et al.~\citep{ghrer2025paramplants} infer grammar-based plant parameters from 3D scans; both assume clean, complete point clouds of isolated plants. CropCraft~\citep{cropcraft2026} fits procedural crop parameters via Bayesian optimization against multi-view NeRF depth maps, and Hadadi et al.~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize,hadadi2025procmaize,nurbsdiff2022} combine vision-language prompting with differentiable NURBS fitting on terrestrial laser scans, requiring roughly ten minutes per isolated plant. Tree-D Fusion~\citep{lee2024treedfusion} reconstructs urban trees from street-view images using diffusion priors, focusing on woody branch envelopes rather than thin crop foliage.

\subsection{Point-Cloud Completion and Slender Geometry}
Point-cloud completion networks have progressed from folding decoders~\citep{yuan2018pcn} to geometry-aware transformers~\citep{yu2021pointr,yu2023adapointr,cai2024einet}, primarily evaluated on compact objects in PCN and ShapeNet-55. While recent methods address amodal completion of occluded plants~\citep{lee2026occ} and thin tubular structures~\citep{curvegaussian2025,qi2026rethinking}, they output unstructured point sets rather than editable parametric models. Moreover, standard Chamfer distance is known to be insensitive to missing thin surfaces~\citep{wu2021dcd,song2026structural,lin2023infocd}, motivating our combined use of Sinkhorn divergence and frozen-latent trait probes.

\subsection{3D Plant Phenotyping Datasets}
Existing 3D plant datasets such as Pheno4D~\citep{schunck2021pheno4d}, Crops3D~\citep{crops3d2024}, and MaizeField3D~\citep{maizefield3d2025} provide valuable scans for organ segmentation and trait estimation~\citep{qi2017pointnet,qi2017pointnetpp,arshad2024nerf_field}. However, none pairs real-field captures and physically simulated row occlusion with exact procedural ground-truth parameters across multiple species, a gap \dataset{} is designed to fill.

\subsection{Multimodal Masked Pretraining and Distillation}
Masked autoencoding~\citep{he2022mae} has been extended to multimodal images~\citep{bachmann2022multimae}, any-to-any generation~\citep{mizrahi2023fourm,bachmann2024fourm21}, point clouds~\citep{pang2022pointmae}, and RGB--depth--point-cloud inputs in EmbodiedMAE~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}. Cross-modal distillation~\citep{hinton2015distillation} enables robust inference when sensors are missing at deployment~\citep{wang2024robustmissing}. By integrating continuous numerical parameter tokens~\citep{golkar2023xval} into generative cross-modal distillation, we uncover how parameter visibility drives modality competition~\citep{peng2022ogm} and gradient interference~\citep{yu2020pcgrad,chen2018gradnorm}.

\subsection{Synthetic Pretraining and Sim-to-Real Transfer}
Synthetic pretraining spans fractal patterns~\citep{kataoka2020fractaldb} to photorealistic procedural scenes~\citep{raistrick2023infinigen,greff2022kubric,tobin2017domainrand}, with downstream utility governed by task alignment~\citep{fan2023intriguing,alemohammad2023mad}. Because \method{} predicts the exact parameter space of the generator and validates transfer on real MaizeField3D captures~\citep{maizefield3d2025}, synthetic pretraining aligns directly with the target task.

\subsection{Training View Distribution}
\label{app:views-full}

In sorghum (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}), restricted models improve slightly on their seen elevations (e.g., $+0.061$ roll $R^2$ on view 0 for the single-view arm) but drop by $0.066\text{--}0.144$ on unseen angles, whereas the all-view model maintains $R^2 = 0.734\text{--}0.778$ across all views. \subsection{Token-Budget Control Arms}
In standard four-stream pretraining, sensor streams share the 122--123 visible token budget with parameter tokens (leaving 107.4 visible sensor tokens vs.\ 117.0 in three-stream models). When we decouple parameter visibility to hold visible sensor tokens fixed at 117.0 across parameter visibility rates of 20\%, 60\%, and 0\% (decoder target-only), sorghum still underperforms the three-stream model on every trait (height $R^2 = 0.959\text{--}0.966$ vs.\ 0.977; leaf roll $0.702\text{--}0.771$ vs.\ 0.781). Conversely, maize improves across all eleven traits at 20\% visibility and 0\% target-only supervision.

\subsection{Parameters as a Decoder Reconstruction Target Only}
Supervising procedural parameters strictly as a decoder target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) degrades sorghum representations relative to omitting the stream entirely (validation $R^2$ drops by $-0.011$ on height, $-0.07$ on leaf roll, $-0.10$ on leaf length, and $-0.15$ on leaf width), concentrating 44\% of latent variance into a single size-correlated principal component ($|r| = 0.95$ with leaf count). \subsection{Distillation Dynamics per Source Modality}
Cross-modal distillation reduces mean generation loss by 30.2\% in sorghum and 43.5\% in maize over 100 epochs, with the largest improvements on single-sensor inputs: sorghum point-cloud-to-cloud Chamfer improves from $5.48$ to $1.39 \times 10^{-3}$, parameter-only-to-cloud Chamfer drops from $22.7$ to $6.83 \times 10^{-3}$, and maize RGB-to-cloud Chamfer drops from $11.3$ to $0.77 \times 10^{-3}$. Moreover, on full validation point clouds, our multi-stream distilled generalist achieves a parameter MAE of 0.019 compared to 0.036 and 0.038 for parameter-only specialist students trained from the same initialization (at learning rates $10^{-4}$ and $3 \times 10^{-5}$), demonstrating the regularizing benefit of joint multimodal reconstruction.

\subsection{Label-Free View Invariance Across Camera Perspectives}
Evaluating prediction consistency across all ten views of each plant provides a label-free measure of view invariance. Distillation reduces cross-view prediction spread by roughly 50\% on observable morphological traits (cutting insertion height error by 59\% and branching angle error by 60\%), whereas unobservable parameters (such as early-release leaf waviness fields with near-zero skill) become more view-sensitive as the decoder fits image noise, motivating their exclusion from the final parameterization.

\subsection{Frozen-Encoder Baselines and Pose Cues}
Paired plant-level comparisons confirm that three-stream \method{} outperforms EmbodiedMAE-B on 8 of 9 sorghum traits (height $+0.005$, roll $+0.04$, leaf length $+0.08$, leaf width $+0.09$), while four-stream \method{} outperforms EmbodiedMAE-B on 10 of 11 maize traits. Under realistic gravity-aligned camera poses (which remove canonical azimuth leakage from synthetic maize renders), Point-MAE height $R^2$ drops from 0.720 to 0.588 while pose-free \method{} wins 5 of 9 decided traits.

\subsection{Point-Cloud Loss}
\label{sec:abl_loss}

To prevent point-cloud decoders from collapsing points onto central stalks, we combine our quality-aware loss (QAL) with a debiased Sinkhorn divergence. Given predicted cloud $\mathcal{P}$, target cloud $\mathcal{T}$, and nearest-neighbor Euclidean distance $d(x,\mathcal{S}) = \min_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \|x-s\|_2$, QAL weights errors smoothly around tolerance $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QAL}} ={}& \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} w\big(d(p,\mathcal{T})\big)\,d(p,\mathcal{T})\\
      &+ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}} w\big(d(t,\mathcal{P})\big)\,d(t,\mathcal{P}),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:qal}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  w(d) = \sigma\big(\alpha\,(d-\tau)\big),
  \label{eq:qal_w}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 100$ and $\tau = 0.01$ ($\approx 10$\,mm at median plant scale). Trained from scratch for 200 epochs on isolated sorghum plants (\cref{tab:loss_single}), QAL alone achieves high precision (0.561) but low recall (0.144), whereas Sinkhorn divergence alone ($S_\varepsilon$ on 1{,}024 points, blur 0.02) achieves higher recall (0.206) but poor precision (0.142). Combining $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QAL}} + 0.5\,S_\varepsilon$ yields the highest $F_1$ at both 5\,mm (0.083) and 10\,mm (0.259), outperforming QAL alone on 82\% of test plants. Hyperparameter screening (\cref{tab:qal_params}) confirms that $\tau \in \{5, 10\}$\,mm and sharp weighting ($\alpha \in \{100, 300\}$) perform best.

\section{Dataset Generation Details}
\label{app:data}

\dataset{} comprises four synthetic subsets: isolated single-plant captures and physically contact-solved simulated rover rows for both maize and sorghum. All renders are generated at $1024 \times 1024$ resolution with a $40^\circ$ vertical field of view ($f_x = f_y = 512 / \tan 20^\circ = 1406.7$\,px, principal point at image center) in OpenGL camera coordinates ($+y$ up, camera looking down $-z$).

\subsection{Procedural Crop Generation}
\noindent\textbf{Maize procedural generator.}
Each of the 15{,}000 maize plants is synthesized from random seed $i$ using a procedural maize model~\citep{vazquez_phenoframe_2026}. Base traits are drawn from Gaussian distributions: plant height $0.832 \pm 0.104$\,m, leaf count $12.35 \pm 2.57$, base leaf length $0.448 \pm 0.057$\,m, base leaf width $0.066 \pm 0.010$\,m, and base insertion angle $43.98^\circ \pm 10.13^\circ$. Traits are coupled through four latent factors (vigor, slenderness, posture, and texture; correlation strength 0.735), canopy position curves (bottom, middle, top), and per-leaf spatial jitter (scale 0.687), producing plant heights from 0.62 to 1.44\,m (median 1.01\,m). Each record stores global height, leaf count, and per-leaf insertion height, length, width, azimuth, insertion angle, and droopiness, with midrib curvature integrated via 10-node Gauss--Legendre quadrature along B-spline centerlines.

\noindent\textbf{Sorghum procedural generator.}
Each of the 15{,}000 sorghum plants is generated from seed $i$ using EvoEngine~\citep{ostermann_sorghum_2024}. Heading angle is sampled uniformly in $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$, stem tilt $0^\circ \pm 1.5^\circ$, leaf count $20 \pm 2$ (clamped to $[2, 128]$), and internode spacing $0.04 \pm 0.03$\,m, yielding stem lengths $\text{internode} \times n_{\text{leaves}} / (1 - s_0)$ between 0.19 and 1.74\,m across 4--35 leaves (mean 19.5). Continuous curves over normalized stem height $t \in [0,1]$ govern leaf insertion height, length, width, alternating roll ($\text{roll}_k = (k \bmod 2) \cdot 180^\circ + \text{roll}(t)$), branching angle, and curvature.

\subsection{Dataset Partitioning}
Both species are split 70\% / 15\% / 15\% into 10{,}500 training, 2{,}250 validation, and 2{,}250 test plants, enriching validation and test splits toward extreme phenotypes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Maize:} 19 morphological features are standardized via median absolute deviation ($z = (x - \mathrm{median}) / (1.4826 \cdot \mathrm{MAD})$) and scored by Mahalanobis distance $\sqrt{z^\top (\Sigma + 10^{-6}I)^{+} z}$. The most extreme plants are assigned alternately to test and validation splits (train scores 1.56--4.60; validation 4.60--23.3; test 4.60--32.2).
  \item \textbf{Sorghum:} Plants are ranked to percentile $r \in [0,1]$ by Mahalanobis distance across six standardized traits (leaf count, stem length, mean and maximum leaf length, and mean and standard deviation of leaf roll). A 30\% evaluation pool is sampled without replacement with probability proportional to $(r + 0.02)^2$ (seed 42) and split equally into validation and test sets (median rank 0.75 vs.\ 0.37 in train), deterministically reproduced by \texttt{data\_split/make\_split.py}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Isolated-Plant Sensor Rendering}
Plants are rendered off-screen in \texttt{pyrender} aimed at bounding-box center $\mathbf{c}$ with 0.15 ambient light and three directional lights from $(1,1,1)$, $(-1,0.5,-0.5)$, and $(0,1,0)$ (intensities 4.0, 2.0, and 1.5) against a neutral grey background (0.5). Metric depth $z \in [n, f]$ is packed losslessly into 32-bit RGBA PNG channels:
\begin{equation}
  v = \left\lfloor \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{z-n}{f-n}, 0, 1\right) (2^{32}-1) \right\rfloor,
\end{equation}
and valid pixels $(u,v)$ back-project to 3D camera coordinates via:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{cam}} = \left( \frac{u - c_x}{f_x} z,\; -\frac{v - c_y}{f_y} z,\; -z \right).
  \label{eq:supp-backproject}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Maize captures.} Ten camera viewpoints are sampled uniformly on the unit sphere at bounding-box-fitted distance:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{dist} = \frac{1.05\,r}{\sin 20^\circ},\quad
  \begin{aligned}
    n &= \max(0.001,\ \mathrm{dist} - 1.5\,r),\\
    f &= \mathrm{dist} + 1.5\,r,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:supp-maizecam}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is half the bounding-box diagonal, with a fourth camera-mounted light (intensity 1.5). Each view outputs RGB, depth, camera pose, procedural parameters, and an 8{,}192-point cloud back-projected from the depth map. Per-leaf curvature is integrated along the rebuilt midrib B-spline over arc length $s \in [0.22, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta = \int_{0.22}^{1} \lvert \kappa(s) \rvert \, ds,
  \qquad \bar\kappa = \theta / S.
  \label{eq:supp-curvature}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Sorghum captures.} Ten viewpoints lie on a fixed Fibonacci sphere rig at 2.5\,m distance ($y_i = 1 - 2(i+0.5)/10$, $\phi_i = i(\sqrt{5}-1)\pi + \epsilon_i$, spanning elevations from $-64^\circ$ to $+64^\circ$), outputting RGB, depth, camera pose, procedural parameters, and complete occlusion-free 8{,}192-point clouds in the camera frame.

\subsection{Simulated Crop Row Rendering}
\noindent\textbf{Row layout and articulated contact solver.}
Each row places target plant $L_i$ between right neighbor $L_{i+1}$ and left neighbor $L_{i+2}$ at stem spacing $d = 0.15$\,m along the $x$-axis across ten row orientations $\theta \in \{0^\circ, 10^\circ, \dots, 90^\circ\}$ with neighbor yaw jitter $\delta \sim U(-15^\circ, 15^\circ)$ (sorghum neighbors are also aligned so their principal SVD stem axes run parallel to the target stem). To prevent unrealistic mesh interpenetration, our articulated contact solver models leaf blades as chains of 8\,cm jointed segments while keeping stems, sheaths (within 2.5\,cm of the stem), and maize tassels rigid.

Contacts are detected via a two-stage \Meets{} check: a 1\,cm dilated voxel grid screen ($\varepsilon_O = 2.8$\,mm, $\varepsilon_M = 6.8$\,mm) followed by exact triangle--triangle intersection tests (allowing surfaces to touch without crossing). As detailed in Algorithms~\ref{alg:supp-bend}--\ref{alg:supp-spacing} and Table~\ref{tab:supp-stages}, target leaves bend only to clear rigid neighbor stems/sheaths, neighbors yield to the target and each other in iterative rounds (including stem-axis rolls up to $\pm 80^\circ$ for blocked sorghum neighbor leaves), and target leaves bend round neighbor foliage only if a neighbor leaf remains boxed in. If collisions persist, joint limits loosen across three stages and stem spacing widens in 5\,cm steps (refined to 1\,cm) up to 1.5\,m. Finally, three settling passes ease every bent leaf back toward its grown shape via 12 binary halvings so leaves rest naturally within a fraction of a millimeter of adjacent foliage. Across all 300{,}000 row orientations (15{,}000 rows $\times$ 10 yaws per species), the solver converged for every orientation, and its final exact triangle--triangle pass accepted each layout only with no inter-plant crossings (Table~\ref{tab:supp-rowstats}). An independent intersection-check suite sharing no code with the solver also found no crossings on the rows it was run on; which rows those were was not recorded, and the bent meshes are not stored with the renders, so that check cannot be repeated on the released data.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{BendClear}: Minimum-deviation collision-free leaf shape}\label{alg:supp-bend}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require leaf $\ell$ with segments $0,\dots,n{-}1$ (collar first); obstacles $\mathcal O$; stage limits $(U,S_0,S,T)$
\State $p \gets$ grown shape (every joint bend $(\alpha_j,\beta_j)=(0,0)$)
\State $j \gets$ first segment of $\ell(p)$ that \Meets{} $\mathcal O$ \Comment{none: clear}
\While{$j$ exists}
  \If{too many steps or test budget spent} \Return \textsc{fail} \EndIf
  \State $q^\star \gets$ none
  \For{$k = j, j{-}1, \dots, \max(0,j{-}3)$} \Comment{joint before contact first}
    \ForAll{bends $(\alpha,\beta)$ of joint $k$, smallest first}
      \State $q \gets p$ with joint $k$ set to $(\alpha,\beta)$, joints after $k$ straightened
      \If{$\sum_i \|(\alpha_i,\beta_i)\| > T$ \textbf{or} segs.\ $k..j$ of $\ell(q)$ \Meets{} $\mathcal O$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
      \EndIf
      \State $q \gets$ least bend between $p$ and $q$ at joint $k$ that clears (3 halvings)
      \If{$\ell(q)$ enters own stem or crosses own plant more than as grown}
        \State \textbf{continue}
      \EndIf
      \State $q^\star \gets q$; \textbf{break} both loops
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \If{$q^\star$ is none} \Return \textsc{fail} \EndIf
  \State $p \gets q^\star$; \quad $j \gets$ first segment $\ge k$ of $\ell(p)$ that \Meets{} $\mathcal O$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $p$
\end{algorithmic}
\noindent\footnotesize For a sorghum neighbor leaf, on \textsc{fail} the search repeats from the grown shape rolled about the stem by $\pm20^\circ,\pm40^\circ,\dots,\pm80^\circ$, keeping the first clear roll.
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{SolveLayout}: Iterative contact resolution for a row layout}\label{alg:supp-layout}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require main plant $P_0$, neighbors $P_1,P_2$ at spacing $d$, angles $\theta$; stage limits
\If{stems or sheaths of any two plants cross} \Return \textsc{fail} \EndIf
\ForAll{leaves $\ell$ of $P_0$ that \Meets{} the stems/sheaths of $P_1,P_2$}
  \State $\ell \gets \textsc{BendClear}(\ell, \text{stems and sheaths of } P_1,P_2)$
  \State \textbf{if} \textsc{fail} \Return \textsc{fail}
\EndFor
\For{round $=1,\dots,8$} \Comment{neighbors give way first}
  \For{$r = 1, 2$}
    \ForAll{leaves $\ell$ of $P_r$ that \Meets{} the other two plants}
      \State $\ell \gets \textsc{BendClear}(\ell, \text{other two plants})$; on \textsc{fail} mark $\ell$ stuck
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
  \If{no leaf changed} \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\For{$t = 1, 2$ \textbf{while} some neighbor leaf is stuck} \Comment{target yields only if necessary}
  \State bend clear the $P_0$ leaves that \Meets{} a stuck leaf; rerun the neighbor rounds
\EndFor
\State \Return \textsc{ok} if no triangle of any plant crosses another plant (exact test), else \textsc{fail}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{SolveOrientation}: Spacing search, stage progression, and settling}\label{alg:supp-spacing}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require plants $P_0,P_1,P_2$; angles $\theta$; $d_0=0.15$\,m, $d_\text{max}=1.5$\,m
\Function{Try}{$d$}
  \For{stage $s=0,1,2$}
    \State $r \gets \textsc{SolveLayout}(P_0,P_1,P_2,d,\theta,\text{limits}_s)$
    \If{$r=\textsc{ok}$ \textbf{or} stems/sheaths cross} \Return $r$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State \Return \textsc{fail}
\EndFunction
\If{\Call{Try}{$d_0$} $=\textsc{ok}$} $d \gets d_0$
\Else
  \State $d\gets$ first of $d_0{+}0.05, d_0{+}0.10,\dots,d_\text{max}$ with \Call{Try}{$d$} $=\textsc{ok}$
  \State $d_\text{lo}\gets$ last failed spacing tried before $d$
  \State $d\gets$ first of $d_\text{lo}{+}0.01, d_\text{lo}{+}0.02,\dots < d$ with \Call{Try}{} $=\textsc{ok}$, else keep $d$
\EndIf
\State settle every bent leaf of the layout kept at $d$ (resting against neighbors)
\State render the input (three plants) and ground truth (target plant alone in the same bent shape)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\noindent\textbf{Rover camera capture and dataset notes.}
Each row orientation is captured by a horizontal rover camera at $(0, h, \mathrm{dist})$ looking at $(0, h, 0)$, where $h$ is the target bounding-box center height ($\mathrm{dist} = 1.0$\,m for maize, $2.5$\,m for sorghum), producing both the three-plant row input (RGB, depth, and 24{,}576-point scene cloud) and the isolated contact-bent target ground truth (8{,}192-point cloud and procedural parameters). Note two minor edge cases in the dataset: (1) at 2.5\,m distance with a $40^\circ$ vertical field of view ($\approx 1.8$\,m vertical span), the top or base of the tallest sorghum plants can extend slightly outside the image frame; and (2) sorghum training plant 0 (generated with seed 0, where EvoEngine does not reseed its RNG) omits parameters for 2 of its 13 leaves and has a mesh 0.35\,m taller than predicted by its stem length, affecting only a single training instance and no validation or test plants.
