\begin{abstract}
Editable, biologically interpretable 3D models of field-grown crops support
high-throughput phenotyping, \emph{in silico} breeding trials, and agronomic
digital twins, but recovering such models from field sensor data remains
difficult: plant organs are slender, neighboring plants occlude one another,
and a field campaign rarely yields more than a single photograph, a noisy
depth frame, or a partial point cloud per plant. Slender geometry under
occlusion is a regime in which learned 3D completion and Chamfer-based
evaluation both fail. In this work, we present \method{}, a multimodal
masked-autoencoder framework that recovers the complete procedural
representation of a plant, the generator parameters encoding leaf count,
insertion heights, blade angles, and surface geometry, from any subset of
RGB, depth, and point-cloud input in a single forward pass.
\upd{The model is pretrained on 15{,}000 procedurally generated plants per
species (maize and sorghum), fine-tuned on rows in which simulated neighbors
occlude the target, and distilled from a full-modality teacher into
single-sensor students.}
\upd{Distillation lowers the cross-modal generation loss by 30\% in sorghum
and 44\% in maize, and halves the sorghum parameter error from a point cloud
alone.}
\upd{Training on rows removes 92\% (maize) and 85\% (sorghum) of the error
that neighbors add for a model trained on isolated plants.}
\upd{Controlled ablations in both species show that exposing most of the
procedural parameters to the encoder degrades phenotype recovery, while using
them only as a reconstruction target degrades the sorghum representation and
improves the maize one.}
We further show that Chamfer distance and linear probes on the frozen
representation rank input modalities differently on slender geometry, and we
adopt the probe as the deciding metric.
\upd{On real field scans with independently reconstructed ground truth,
\method{} \todo{matches} the accuracy of a staged annotation pipeline and
reduces the time required for procedural reconstruction from hours of manual
specification, or minutes of pipeline computation, to milliseconds per
plant.}
\upd{We will release \dataset{}, the 30{,}000-plant, two-species benchmark
behind these experiments, which pairs multimodal field captures under
simulated row occlusion with exact procedural ground truth.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% Fig. 1 (teaser) lives in the title block of main.tex, full width on page 1.

\upd{Accurate three-dimensional models of individual crop plants enable
high-throughput phenotyping of morphological traits such as leaf angle,
internode length, and canopy architecture, and they are the basis of emerging
agricultural digital twins in which breeding trials run \emph{in
silico}~\citep{hadadi2025procmaize, maizefield3d2025}.} The representation
best suited to these uses is procedural: a compact set of biologically
interpretable parameters that a generator expands into complete, editable,
simulation-ready geometry~\citep{prusin1990abop, floraforge2026}.
\upd{A procedural plant occupies a few kilobytes where a laser scan occupies
megabytes, every parameter corresponds to a trait used by agronomists, and
the model can be edited, re-rendered, and perturbed in ways that unstructured
geometry does not support.}
\upd{Field crops also instantiate a challenge of general interest in 3D
computer vision: recovering a parametric model of a thin, heavily occluded
object from partial multimodal observations.}

Yet recovering procedural structure from field-acquired sensor data
remains a significant challenge, and every existing route presupposes a
rich, clean observation. Manual parameter specification takes hours per
plant~\citep{floraforge2026}. Staged reconstruction pipelines automate
the process; a recent system annotates laser scans with a
vision-language model and fits a procedural descriptor to
5.4\,mm median accuracy, but it requires a complete scan of an
isolated plant and roughly ten minutes of computation per
plant~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize}.
\upd{Per-scene optimization methods search generator parameters against
multi-view imagery~\citep{cropcraft2026}, and recent learned models infer
parametric plants, but only from a clean, complete point
cloud~\citep{demeter2025, ghrer2025paramplants}.}
\upd{A phenotyping campaign produces the opposite: thousands of plants per
day, each observed by whichever sensor was practical (a camera, a depth
frame, or a partial scan).}

\upd{In addition, crop plants are slender: a maize blade is centimeters wide,
millimeters thick, and up to a meter long.}
\upd{In a planted row, each plant is further occluded by its neighbors, whose
leaves interleave with the target; in our field simulations, a third of the
target's pixels and two thirds of the scene's point cloud belong to
neighboring plants.} This regime violates the
assumptions of learned 3D completion: the standard benchmarks contain
compact, largely convex objects and no slender-under-occlusion
category~\citep{yuan2018pcn,yu2021pointr}, and Chamfer distance, the
default metric, is structurally insensitive to thin sheets, so it can
reward reconstructions that miss entire blades~\citep{wu2021dcd,
song2026structural}. Occlusion-aware completion of thin plant organs has
begun to address the input side of this regime, but its output is a
completed point cloud rather than structure~\citep{lee2026occ}.
To our knowledge, no prior method recovers a procedural, re-renderable
representation of slender crop organs from a single field-practical
observation (a photograph, a depth frame, or a partial point cloud)
under row-neighbor occlusion: methods that infer plant
parameters require clean, complete scans of isolated
plants~\citep{demeter2025,ghrer2025paramplants}, and methods that
tolerate field occlusion complete unstructured geometry
instead~\citep{lee2026occ}.

In this work, we present \method{}, a framework that makes this inverse
problem tractable by using the procedural generator itself as the prior.
\upd{We pretrain a four-stream masked
autoencoder~\citep{he2022mae,bachmann2022multimae,mizrahi2023fourm,
dong2025embodiedmae} over RGB, depth, point clouds, and tokenized
procedural parameters of 15{,}000 generated plants per species, maize and
sorghum, and fine-tune it on simulated rows in which neighboring leaves bend
on contact.} Because random modality subsets are masked during pretraining,
a single model learns to complete any subset at inference.
\upd{A full-modality teacher is then distilled~\citep{hinton2015distillation}
into single-sensor students for deployment.}
\upd{Distillation lowers the cross-modal generation loss by 30\% in sorghum
and 44\% in maize, and it halves the sorghum parameter error from a point
cloud alone.}
\upd{Row fine-tuning against the target plant behind its neighbors removes
most of the error that the neighbors add: 92\% in maize and 85\% in
sorghum.}

\upd{We also address a design question that arises for any model with access
to generative structure: where should the parameter stream enter?}
\upd{In our controlled ablations, a parameter stream that is largely visible
to the encoder harms the representation in both species.}
\upd{With the shared token budget, adding the parameter stream to the encoder
degrades the frozen representation on every sorghum phenotype target, and on
maize the paired comparison is a draw.}
\upd{Control arms that restore the sensor-token budget separate the species:
in sorghum the loss persists, even when the parameters serve only as a
reconstruction target for the decoder, while in maize the arms that see
20\,\% of the parameters or only reconstruct them improve every trait.}
\upd{Supervising parameters as a generation target, however, lets the
distilled generalist outperform a parameter-only specialist at parameter
regression} \updtwo{(recipe MAE from the point cloud 0.019 against 0.036 on the
final data).}
\upd{One interpretation, consistent with modality
competition~\citep{peng2022ogm} and negative transfer~\citep{yu2020pcgrad},
is that a stream that already contains the answer suppresses learning in the
streams that must provide the trait information at test time.}

\upd{Adding depth to the photograph and the point cloud lowers Chamfer
distance by 28\% in sorghum and 25\% in maize, whereas a linear probe on the
frozen representation, read out on phenotypic traits, finds little or no gain
from depth and identifies RGB as the main source of trait information.}
On slender geometry the two metrics measure
different quantities, surface proximity and recovered structure, and we
adopt the probe as the deciding metric for modality
decisions~\citep{chen2020simclr,wu2021dcd}.

\upd{We claim that fragmentary field sensing suffices to recover a complete,
interpretable procedural plant, provided the generator is used for both the
training data and the output representation.}
\upd{We validate this claim on real field scans of maize and sorghum with
independently reconstructed ground
truth~\citep{maizefield3d2025,hadadi2026vlmmaize}, including rows in which
neighbors occlude the target, \todo{demonstrating accuracy matching staged
annotation pipelines while running in milliseconds per plant}.}
The key contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Task and framework.} \upd{The first formulation and system,
  to our knowledge, for recovering procedural plant representations of
  slender, row-occluded field crops from \emph{any subset} of RGB, depth,
  and point-cloud input in one amortized forward pass, with full-modal to
  single-sensor distillation (generation loss $-30\%$ in sorghum and
  $-44\%$ in maize; sorghum parameter error from a point cloud alone
  halved), and row fine-tuning that removes most of the error added by
  neighboring plants (92\% in maize, 85\% in sorghum).}
  \item \textbf{A design rule for structured modalities.} \upd{Controlled,
  budget-matched, two-species evidence that a parameter stream largely
  visible to the encoder degrades the sensor representation in both
  species, while the value of the parameters as a reconstruction target
  depends on the generator (it degrades the sorghum representation and
  improves the maize one); we expect this guidance to transfer to any
  multimodal model with access to privileged generative structure.}
  \item \textbf{An evaluation protocol for slender geometry.} \upd{A
  demonstration that Chamfer distance and downstream phenotype probes
  disagree systematically on slender organs, and a frozen-latent probe
  protocol that resolves modality decisions where Chamfer is insensitive.}
  \item \textbf{A benchmark.} \upd{\dataset{}, a two-species procedural
  field benchmark of 30{,}000 plants spanning diverse phenotypes, generator
  configurations, and row occlusion severities, with paired multimodal
  captures, physically simulated row occlusion, and exact parameter ground
  truth, together with a real-field evaluation split carrying reconstructed
  procedural ground truth across the genotypes of a diversity
  panel~\citep{maizefield3d2025}.} No existing dataset combines these
  elements (Tab.~\ref{tab:datacompare})~\citep{schunck2021pheno4d,yuan2018pcn}.
  We will release the data, generation code, and evaluation scripts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Recovering procedural plants.}
\upd{Inverse procedural modeling has a long history for woody plants:
parameters of tree generators have been recovered by stochastic
search~\citep{stava2014ipmtrees}, by Markov chain Monte Carlo
inference~\citep{talton2011metropolis}, and by inferring L-systems from point
clouds~\citep{guo2020ipmlsys,latentlsys2023}.} These methods optimize or
infer per instance. Recent systems address crops specifically. Demeter
learns a parametric morphology model from soybean point
clouds~\citep{demeter2025}, Ghrer et al.\ infer grammar-style plant
representations from 3D scans~\citep{ghrer2025paramplants}, CropCraft fits a
procedural crop model by Bayesian optimization against multi-view
imagery~\citep{cropcraft2026}, and a recent staged pipeline reconstructs
procedural maize from terrestrial laser scans through vision-language-model
annotation and differentiable NURBS
refinement~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize,nurbsdiff2022}. Each of these methods
presupposes a single rich observation, a clean and complete point cloud or a
multi-view capture of an isolated plant, and none addresses missing
modalities or inter-plant occlusion.

\paragraph{Programs from observations.}
Outside agriculture, decoding parametric programs from observations is an
established pattern: neural shape programs~\citep{shapeprograms2019},
diffusion-based inverse procedural content generation from a single
image~\citep{zhao2025dipcg}, text-conditioned CAD
generation~\citep{text2cad2024}, and cadrille, which maps images, point
clouds, or text to executable CAD models~\citep{kolodiazhnyi2025cadrille}.
These systems operate on rigid objects observed cleanly and in isolation.
Our setting differs in the geometry (deformable, slender organs), in the
input (noisy, occluded field sensors), and in a design question that none of
them raises: whether the program parameters should be visible to the encoder
at all.

\paragraph{Completion and slender geometry.}
\upd{Learned point cloud completion has progressed from folding-based
decoders~\citep{yuan2018pcn} to geometry-aware
transformers~\citep{yu2021pointr,yu2023adapointr,cai2024einet}, trained on
benchmarks that contain compact, largely convex objects and no
slender-under-occlusion category.} Completion of thin plant organs under
occlusion has emerged recently~\citep{lee2026occ}, and reconstruction of
thin structures from clean multi-view data is an active
topic~\citep{curvegaussian2025,qi2026rethinking}, but in both lines the
output is unstructured geometry rather than an editable model. Chamfer
distance, the standard completion metric, is insensitive to thin sheets and
can be reduced by collapsing predictions onto bulk
structure~\citep{wu2021dcd,song2026structural,lin2023infocd}; we therefore
evaluate with trait probes alongside geometric metrics
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}).

\paragraph{Multimodal masked pretraining.}
\upd{Masked autoencoding~\citep{he2022mae} extends to multiple modalities in
MultiMAE~\citep{bachmann2022multimae}, to any-to-any generation over
tokenized modalities in 4M~\citep{mizrahi2023fourm,bachmann2024fourm21}, to
point clouds in Point-MAE~\citep{pang2022pointmae}, and to combined RGB,
depth, and point cloud inputs in EmbodiedMAE, on which our architecture is
based~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}.} Robustness to missing modalities is a
recognized concern~\citep{wang2024robustmissing}, and distillation from
multi-sensor teachers to single-sensor students is established for
discriminative tasks~\citep{hinton2015distillation}. \upd{We extend these
methods in two ways: we add a structured generative modality, encoded with
continuous number tokens~\citep{golkar2023xval}, and we distill a generative
full-modality teacher for structured parameter regression.}

\paragraph{Learning from procedural data.}
\upd{Pretraining on purely synthetic data is viable, from abstract
fractals~\citep{kataoka2020fractaldb} to procedurally generated
scenes~\citep{raistrick2023infinigen,greff2022kubric}, with domain
randomization reducing the gap to real data~\citep{tobin2017domainrand} and
with the value of synthetic data known to depend on the downstream
task~\citep{fan2023intriguing}.} Our setting is the favorable case for this
strategy: the procedural generator defines the output representation, spans
the morphology of the target crop, and is paired with real field evaluation
data~\citep{maizefield3d2025}. \upd{Our results add one caveat to this
strategy: the generator's parameters are valuable as data, but exposing most
of them to the encoder reduces representation quality in both species, and
their value as a reconstruction target depends on the generator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params}).}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\upd{In this section, we introduce \method{}, a unified multimodal
masked-autoencoder framework designed to recover editable procedural plant
models from fragmentary and occluded field observations.} \upd{We begin by
formulating the procedural inverse reconstruction problem
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-form}).} \upd{We then describe our synthetic field-data
engine, detailing how procedurally generated crop plants and physically
simulated row occlusion scenes are created (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-data}).} \upd{Next, we explain the four-stream tokenization, dynamic Dirichlet masking
schedule, and multi-term reconstruction loss functions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-pretrain}).} \upd{Finally, we present our teacher--student
distillation strategy for deployment on single sensors
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-distill}) and detail our experimental evaluation and
domain transfer protocols (Sec.~\ref{sec:field}).} \upd{An overall schematic of
the framework is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}.}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{\upd{\textbf{\method{} framework overview.} (a) \textbf{Four-stream
  pretraining}: Tokenized streams for RGB images, metric depth frames, 3D
  point clouds, and continuous procedural parameters enter a shared ViT
  encoder under a dynamic Dirichlet masking budget.} \upd{Modality-specific
  decoder heads reconstruct all target streams.} \upd{During pretraining,

\subsection{Problem formulation: inverse procedural reconstruction}
\label{sec:method-form}

\upd{A procedural plant generator $G$ is a generative function that maps a
continuous vector of parameter attributes $\theta \in \R^{d_\theta}$ to an
editable, full-resolution 3D triangular mesh $G(\theta)$.} \upd{The vector
$\theta$ acts as a complete descriptor of the generated plant: it encodes
macro-level plant structure (such as stem height and internode spacing) as
well as organ-level morphology for every leaf slot (including insertion
height, blade length and width, and insertion, roll, and azimuth angles).}

\upd{In real-world agricultural field settings, sensors yield partial, noisy,
and occluded observations.} \upd{Let
$\mathcal{M} = \{\mathrm{rgb}, \mathrm{d}, \mathrm{pc}\}$ represent the
universe of available field sensor modalities: a 2D RGB photograph, a
single depth frame, and an unstructured 3D point cloud, respectively.} \upd{In
any given field campaign, a user may acquire only an incomplete subset
$S \subseteq \mathcal{M}$ (for example, a single photograph taken from a
mobile device or a partial point cloud captured by a tractor-mounted depth
camera).} \upd{Furthermore, neighboring plants in a crop row routinely interleave
their leaves, causing heavy inter-plant occlusion.}

\upd{Our primary goal is to learn an amortized feed-forward inverse mapping
$f_\phi$:}
\begin{equation}
  f_\phi\!\left(\{x^m : m \in S\}\right)
  = \left(\hat\theta, \{\hat x^m : m \in \mathcal{M}\}\right),
  \label{eq:procedural-inverse}
\end{equation}
\upd{where $\phi$ denotes trainable network parameters, $\hat\theta$ is the
predicted procedural description, and $\hat x^m$ represents the completed
3D geometry and visual modalities.}

\paragraph{Intuition behind the formulation.}
\upd{Traditional 3D completion methods attempt to complete missing geometry by
outputting raw point clouds or voxel grids.} \upd{However, slender crop organs
under row occlusion present an ill-posed inverse problem: when a leaf blade
is hidden behind a neighbor, raw geometric completion can only guess point
locations.} \upd{In contrast, by predicting the underlying procedural parameters
$\hat\theta$, \method{} uses the structure of the generator $G$ as a strong
generative prior.} \upd{Predicting $\hat\theta$ guarantees that the output mesh
$G(\hat\theta)$ is structurally complete, editable, and interpretable in
terms of physiological traits used by agronomists.} \upd{Furthermore, while
per-plant numerical optimization pipelines require minutes to hours per
scan, our feed-forward network $f_\phi$ executes the inversion in a single
forward pass taking milliseconds.}

\paragraph{Procedural plant generation.}
\upd{We build generators for two major agricultural crop species with distinct
structural dynamics: maize (\emph{Zea mays}) and sorghum (\emph{Sorghum
bicolor}).}
\begin{itemize}
\upd{  \item \textbf{Maize generator}: Produces plants with heights ranging from
  0.62\,m to 1.44\,m and leaf counts from 3 to 22.} \upd{Morphological parameters
  are sampled from continuous Gaussian distributions coupled through four
  latent factors (vigor, slenderness, posture, and texture).} \upd{This sampling
  leaves leaf count and plant height weakly correlated ($r = 0.20$).} \upd{\item \textbf{Sorghum generator}: Uses the EvoEngine sorghum generator to
  produce plants with stem heights ranging from 0.19\,m to 1.74\,m and leaf
  counts from 4 to 35, with stem length strongly coupled to leaf count
  ($\text{stem length} = 0.05\,n_{\text{leaves}} - 0.001$, $R^2 = 0.988$).}
\end{itemize}
\upd{Including both species enables us to evaluate whether our framework
generalizes across both strongly coupled and weakly coupled morphological
regimes.}

\paragraph{Isolated-plant sensor rendering.}
\upd{For each generated plant instance, we render ten camera perspectives at
$1024 \times 1024$ resolution ($40^\circ$ vertical field of view).} \upd{For each
perspective, we export a standard 2D RGB photograph, a metric depth frame
stored losslessly as a 32-bit packed file, and camera calibration
parameters.} \upd{Sorghum captures use a fixed 10-camera Fibonacci sphere rig
positioned at a distance of 2.5\,m (spanning elevation angles from
$-64^\circ$ to $+64^\circ$), and each view carries the occlusion-free
whole-plant point cloud in the camera frame.} \upd{Maize captures draw ten
uniform random spherical perspectives with camera distances adapted to the
plant's bounding box, generating partial 8{,}192-point clouds
back-projected directly from depth images to mimic surface-only camera
captures.}

\paragraph{Physically simulated row neighbor occlusion.}
\upd{In agricultural fields, plants grow in dense rows where leaves physically
touch and overlap.} \upd{To model this occlusion, we construct simulated crop
rows placing each target plant between two adjacent neighbors from the same
dataset split at a realistic 0.15\,m stem spacing.} \upd{Rows are rendered across
ten yaw orientation angles ($\theta \in \{0^\circ, 10^\circ, \dots,
90^\circ\}$) with uniform random neighbor orientation offsets
($\pm 15^\circ$).}

\upd{A physical contact solver resolves inter-plant leaf interpenetration while
preserving natural shapes.} \upd{Each leaf blade is modeled as an articulated
chain of 8\,cm segments connected by joints that bend along circular arcs.} \upd{When two leaves collide, the solver applies exact triangle--triangle
intersection checks and rotates joints by the minimum angle required to
clear contact.} \upd{Neighbors bend first; the main target plant bends only when
neighbors reach their joint rotation limits.}

\upd{A ground-rover camera captures full row scenes (yielding an RGB photograph,
depth frame, and a 24{,}576-point scene cloud containing both target and
neighbor geometry) alongside ground-truth target files containing the main
target plant alone in its contact-bent shape (8{,}192 points).} \upd{In maize
rows, neighboring leaves account for 59--68\% of the plant pixels in the
frame and two thirds of the input point cloud; in sorghum rows, 23\% of the
target plant's pixels are hidden behind neighboring leaves on average
across orientation angles.}

\paragraph{Dataset partitioning.}
\upd{Plants are split into training (70\%, 10{,}500 plants), validation (15\%,
2{,}250 plants), and test sets (15\%, 2{,}250 plants) by plant instance.} \upd{Validation and test splits are enriched with extreme morphological
outliers, selected via Mahalanobis distance ranking across core structural
features.}

\subsection{Four-stream masked pretraining}
\label{sec:method-pretrain}

\paragraph{Multimodal tokenization.}
\upd{Our network architecture builds on EmbodiedMAE~\cite{dong2025embodiedmae},
extending multi-sensor visual encoders to ingest structured procedural
parameters alongside standard vision modalities:}
\begin{enumerate}
\upd{  \item \textbf{RGB and depth patches}: 2D RGB photographs and metric depth
  maps are partitioned into 196 non-overlapping patches on a $14 \times 14$
  grid.} \upd{Patches are linearly projected into 768-dimensional token
  embeddings added to 2D sine--cosine spatial positional embeddings.} \upd{\item \textbf{Point cloud groups}: Following
  Point-MAE~\cite{pang2022pointmae}, 3D point clouds are subsampled to 196
  group centers using farthest point sampling (FPS).} \upd{For each center, its
  32 nearest spatial neighbors are grouped and processed by a lightweight
  PointNet encoder~\cite{qi2017pointnet} to generate a 768-dimensional
  token.} \upd{\item \textbf{Procedural parameter tokens ($\theta$)}: The parameter
  vector $\theta$ is formatted as $1 + K$ continuous numerical tokens: 1
  token for global plant-level attributes and $K$ tokens corresponding to
  individual leaf slots ($K=28$ for maize, $K=24$ for sorghum; plants with
  more leaves keep their first $K$ slots).} \upd{Rather than discretizing
  continuous numbers into string text, attributes are affinely normalized
  to $[0,1]$ and projected linearly into embedding space as continuous
  numerical tokens via xVal-style encoding~\cite{golkar2023xval}.}
\end{enumerate}
\upd{Learned 1D modality embeddings are added to distinguish streams, and a
learnable \texttt{[CLS]} token is prepended to the input sequence.} \upd{The
sequence is processed by a 12-block ViT encoder (width 768) followed by an
8-block decoder (width 512).} \upd{The decoder restores all $L$ token positions
with learnable mask tokens; linear heads predict RGB and depth patches, a
FoldingNet head emits 41 points per point-cloud token, and an MLP regresses
the continuous parameters.} \upd{Leaf presence is not predicted per slot. When
predicted parameters are scored against the ground truth, the real leaf slots
are selected by the ground-truth validity mask; when a recipe is used without
ground truth (Tab.~\ref{tab:regime1}), the leaf count is read from the predicted
recipe itself: maize stores it in the plant token, and for sorghum it follows
from the predicted stem length through the generator's rule (stem length
$=0.05n-0.001$), and the first $n$ leaf slots are kept.}

\paragraph{Dynamic Dirichlet modality masking.}
\upd{To train a single network capable of completing any arbitrary subset of
input modalities at test time, we apply a heavy overall token masking
budget of $\rho = 0.80$.} \upd{The total number of visible tokens $V$ is given
by:}
\begin{equation}
  V = \left\lfloor (1-\rho)\,L \right\rfloor,
  \label{eq:visible-budget}
\end{equation}
\upd{yielding $V=122$ visible tokens for sorghum ($L=613$ total tokens) and
$V=123$ for maize ($L=617$).}

\upd{At each pretraining step, $V$ is allocated across input streams by sampling
a proportion vector from a symmetric Dirichlet distribution,
$\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\mathbf{1})$.} \upd{The sampling is constrained such that
every stream retains at least one visible token and at least 25\% masked
tokens.} \upd{Because parameter tokens are included within this budget in the
headline setup, the parameter stream remains $\sim$58\% visible on average
during pretraining.}

\upd{To analyze parameter stream placement (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params}), we also
train control arms where parameter tokens are uncoupled from the sensor
budget and masked at fixed visibility ratios $\rho_\theta$:}
\begin{equation}
 V_\theta =
 \begin{cases}
  0, & \rho_\theta = 1,\\
  \max\!\left(1, \left\lfloor (1+K)(1-\rho_\theta) \right\rfloor\right), & 0 \le \rho_\theta < 1.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:parameter-visibility}
\end{equation}
\upd{Setting $\rho_\theta = 1$ forces procedural parameters to act strictly as a
decoder reconstruction target without entering the encoder.}

\paragraph{Multi-term reconstruction objective.}
\upd{The pretraining objective optimizes four modality-specific reconstruction
loss terms:}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} = \ell_{\mathrm{rgb}} + \ell_{\mathrm{d}} + \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} + 5\ell_\theta.
  \label{eq:reconstruction-loss}
\end{equation}
\upd{Here, $\ell_{\mathrm{rgb}}$ is MSE computed on patch-normalized RGB
pixels~\cite{he2022mae}, $\ell_{\mathrm{d}}$ is MSE on min--max normalized
depth values, and $\ell_\theta$ is Smooth-$L_1$ loss evaluated over masked
parameter tokens.}

\upd{For 3D point clouds, standard nearest-neighbor Chamfer distance allows
multiple predicted points to match a single target point without penalty,
encouraging decoders to collapse points into dense clumps on central stems
while ignoring slender leaf blades~\cite{wu2021dcd,song2026structural}.} \upd{To
address this limitation, we supervise point cloud prediction using a
sigmoid-weighted Chamfer loss:}
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} = \frac{1}{|\hat P|}\sum_{p\in\hat P}w(\delta(p,P))\,\delta(p,P) + \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{q\in P}w(\delta(q,\hat P))\,\delta(q,\hat P),
 \label{eq:point-loss}
\end{equation}
\upd{where $\delta(p,P) = \min_{q \in P} \|p - q\|_2$,
$w(r) = \sigma(a(r - \tau))$, with slope parameter $a = 100$ and spatial
threshold $\tau = 0.01$ (relative to unit-sphere radius, $\approx 7$\,mm on
a 1.4\,m plant).}

\upd{To further penalize missing leaf blades, our headline pretraining setup
incorporates an optimal-transport term based on debiased Sinkhorn
divergence $S_\varepsilon$~\cite{feydy2019sinkhorn}:}
\begin{equation}
  \ell_{\mathrm{pc}}^{\star} = \ell_{\mathrm{pc}} + 0.5\,S_{\varepsilon}\!\left(\hat P_{1024}, P_{1024}\right),
  \label{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}
\end{equation}
\upd{computed between random 1{,}024-point subsets of predicted and target
point clouds ($\varepsilon = 0.02$).} \upd{Sinkhorn divergence matches point sets
as global probability distributions, forcing the decoder to cover the full
spatial extent of slender leaf organs.} \upd{The ablation arms of
Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments} use Eq.~\eqref{eq:point-loss} alone; the
Sinkhorn term enters the headline model and the distillation.} \upd{Models are
trained with AdamW ($\beta = (0.9, 0.95)$, weight decay 0.05, cosine
decay): the headline schedule peaks at $6 \times 10^{-4}$ over 1{,}000
epochs of all ten views at global batch 256, while ablation arms train for
a matched budget of 197{,}400 optimizer steps at global batch 32, one view
per plant per epoch, peaking at $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$.}

\subsection{Full-modality teacher to single-sensor students}
\label{sec:distill}\label{sec:method-distill}

\upd{While pretraining exposes the encoder to partial subsets of all four
modalities, single-sensor field deployment (e.g., inputting only a single
photograph) presents a configuration pretraining never shows the encoder:
every token of the unobserved streams absent.} \upd{To close this gap, we employ
teacher--student feature distillation~\cite{hinton2015distillation}.}

\paragraph{Distillation mechanism.}
\upd{The teacher model is a frozen four-stream network receiving all input
modalities, with parameter tokens as privileged information: for sorghum
every token of every stream is visible, while for maize the teacher retains
half the tokens of each stream, since its point-cloud reconstruction
degrades when fully visible.} \upd{The teacher outputs a global encoder latent
$c^T \in \R^{768}$ and normalized decoder feature maps
$F^T \in \R^{L \times 512}$.}

\upd{Student models are initialized from teacher weights and trained for 100
epochs using AdamW (learning rate $10^{-4}$, global batch 128).} \upd{During
student training, 80\% of steps perform single-source distillation where
the student receives only a single source
$s \in \{\mathrm{rgb}, \mathrm{d}, \mathrm{pc}, \theta\}$, while 20\% of
steps perform standard four-stream pretraining to maintain feature
stability.} \upd{At deployment only sensor sources are available, and each is
evaluated alone.} \upd{The student is optimized via combined reconstruction and
cross-modal feature alignment:}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{student}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}} + \frac{\lambda_F}{|\mathcal{J}_{\neg s}|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}_{\neg s}} \|F^S_j - F^T_j\|_2^2 + \lambda_c \|c^S - c^T\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:distillation-loss}
\end{equation}
\upd{where $\mathcal{J}_{\neg s}$ denotes decoder token positions belonging to
unobserved non-source streams ($\lambda_F = 1.0, \lambda_c = 0.5$).} \upd{Through
Eq.~\eqref{eq:distillation-loss}, single-sensor student models learn to
reconstruct complete 3D geometry and continuous procedural parameters
guided by multi-sensor teacher latents.}

\subsection{Field evaluation and transfer protocols}
\label{sec:field}

\upd{We evaluate model performance across three operational regimes:}

\paragraph{1. Phenotypic trait probing.}
\upd{To measure phenotypic trait information captured within encoder latents
without relying on decoders, we fit linear ridge regression probes on
frozen \texttt{[CLS]} tokens of the training plants and score them on the
held-out validation and test plants, one deterministic view per plant.} \upd{Procedural parameter inputs are zeroed during probing to prevent label
leakage.} \upd{Trait recovery is evaluated using the coefficient of determination
($R^2$) accompanied by 95\% paired bootstrap confidence intervals (2{,}000
resamples over plants).} \upd{A model modification is classified as beneficial
only when its performance gain confidence interval excludes zero on both
validation and test splits.} \upd{Under partial point-cloud input, the probe is
either refit per visibility condition (\emph{matched}) or applied unchanged
from the complete-cloud fit (\emph{transferred}); partial-cloud
reconstruction keeps the masked input regime of pretraining.}

\paragraph{2. Row occlusion protocol.}
\upd{Models are evaluated on simulated crop rows under two configurations:}
\begin{itemize}
\upd{  \item \textbf{Zero-shot transfer}: Feeding row observations directly into
  isolated-trained models to evaluate sensitivity to neighbor occlusion.} \upd{\item \textbf{Row fine-tuning}: Training decoders to reconstruct target
  main plants behind occluding neighbors while inputting full row scene
  observations.}
\end{itemize}
\upd{A control arm runs the same fine-tuning schedule on the isolated twins, and
generated clouds are scored against the target plant and against the input
scene, so a model that merely copies the scene is caught.}

\paragraph{3. Real-field validation.}
\upd{Real-field performance is evaluated on field captures from the MaizeField3D
dataset~\citep{maizefield3d2025}.} \upd{Predicted procedural parameters and
re-rendered 3D meshes are compared against the procedural fits of a staged
vision-language pipeline~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize}, which serve as
independently reconstructed ground truth.}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}\label{sec:experiments}

\upd{In this section, we present an extensive experimental evaluation of
\method{}.} \upd{We describe our experimental setup (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-setup}),
followed by evaluations across three deployment regimes: isolated
single-view reconstruction (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-regime1}), difficulty
progression across viewpoint and self-occlusion (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-regime2}),
and neighbor occlusion in realistic field rows
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-occlusion}).} \upd{We then analyze cross-modal distillation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-main}), investigate the placement of the procedural
parameter stream (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params}), uncover systematic
divergences between geometric metrics and downstream trait probes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}), describe the real-field validation protocol
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-real}), and analyze scaling, cross-species transfer, and
latent space structure (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-repr}).}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:exp-setup}

\paragraph{Models and compute budgets.}
\upd{To establish controlled comparisons across ablations, all variant models
share a standardized step budget of 197{,}400 optimizer updates at a global
batch size of 32 (corresponding to 600 epochs sampling one random camera
view per plant over the 10{,}500 training plants).} \upd{All controlled ablation
variants use identical view-sampling random seeds and a ViT-Base encoder
(114\,M parameters) unless model scale is explicitly varied.} \upd{Experimental
arms that vary the volume of training plants adjust epoch counts
proportionally to maintain an identical step budget
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-pretrain}).}

\upd{The headline full-modality model is trained on an extended schedule of
1{,}000 epochs across all ten camera views at a global batch size of 256,
incorporating the Sinkhorn optimal-transport point cloud loss
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-pretrain}).} \upd{The headline model encodes the procedural
parameter stream within its shared Dirichlet masking budget and serves as
the weight initialization for all teacher and student models in
Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-main}.} \upd{Results are reported on the validation split
unless stated otherwise.}

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
\upd{We evaluate 3D geometric accuracy using symmetric Chamfer distance on
squared point displacements in the unit-sphere frame
($\times 10^{-3}$; \updtwo{a value of 1.0 corresponds to
a root-mean-square displacement of $\approx 33$\,mm on the median sorghum
plant and $\approx 19$\,mm on the median maize plant, whose metric scales we
measure per plant on the raw camera-frame clouds}).} \upd{For pretrained models, Chamfer distance is evaluated in the
masked input regime of pretraining; for distilled student models, it is
evaluated with unmasked sensor inputs.}

\upd{Parameter regression accuracy is quantified via mean absolute error (MAE)
evaluated over normalized $[0,1]$ continuous fields of hidden, non-padding
leaf parameter tokens.} \upd{Where per-trait relative performance is informative,
we report trait \emph{skill}, defined as}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Skill} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{MAE}}{\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{mean}}},
  \label{eq:trait-skill}
\end{equation}
\upd{where $1.0$ indicates perfect parameter recovery, $0.0$ represents
performance equivalent to predicting the dataset mean, and negative values
indicate predictions worse than the mean baseline.}

\upd{Phenotypic trait representation quality is evaluated with linear ridge
regression probes fit on frozen encoder \texttt{[CLS]} tokens with all
procedural parameter inputs zeroed to prevent label leakage.} \upd{Probes are fit
on a single deterministic view per training plant and evaluated on held-out
validation and test sets (2{,}250 validation, 2{,}250 test plants).} \upd{Performance is reported as coefficient of determination ($R^2$) accompanied
by 95\% paired bootstrap confidence intervals (2{,}000 resamples over
plants).} \upd{A model modification is classified as a \emph{win} only when its
gain's confidence interval excludes zero on both validation and test splits
simultaneously.}

\paragraph{Species developmental traits.}
\upd{In sorghum, four core developmental traits have effective rank two due to
generator allometry (height and leaf count are tightly coupled,
$r = 0.994$).} \upd{We therefore supplement sorghum trait probing with
independent parameter axes varied by the generator, including leaf roll and
leaf blade length.} \upd{In maize, eleven largely independent morphological
traits are evaluated directly (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-data}).}

one leaf for $91$ to $100\%$ of plants, the height is within $2$ to $5\%$, and
the mean leaf-parameter skill is $0.66$ to $0.68$ in sorghum and $0.57$ to
$0.59$ in maize.}
\upd{The skill is high on the leaf's position along the stem ($0.93$ to
$0.97$ in sorghum, $0.83$ in maize) and on its angle ($0.89$ to $0.95$;
$0.76$ to $0.79$), moderate on length ($0.62$ to $0.70$), and lowest on sorghum blade width
($0.20$ to $0.22$), whose spread between plants is small.}
\upd{The headline model given all three streams is no better than the
generalist given one, because it was never trained to read every token of its
input.}
\upd{On the first release, three waviness fields were unrecoverable from any
source (skill $-0.04$ to $0.02$); the final generator does not include them.}

Figure~\ref{fig:qual} illustrates qualitative single-photograph
reconstructions on held-out test plants.} \upd{While the headline model before
distillation generates diffuse point clouds around target silhouettes, the
distilled generalist model resolves individual leaf blades, accurately
recovering normalized leaf length profiles along the central stem from base
to apex.} \upd{Furthermore, because the output representation is procedural,
predicted parameters can be interactively modified (such as adjusting
individual blade insertion angles) and re-rendered to create editable,
simulation-ready 3D models.}

\subsection{Regime II: viewpoint elevation, self-occlusion, and complexity}
\label{sec:regime2}\label{sec:exp-regime2}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

  difficulty.} (A) Photograph-to-cloud Chamfer distance of test plants
  plotted by camera elevation angle (208--244 plants per elevation view).} \upd{Pretrained and distilled single-sensor models follow a symmetric U-shaped
  error curve centered on horizontal side views; camera elevation accounts
  for $\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$ of per-plant reconstruction variance.} \upd{(B) Performance gain from masking 50\% of source tokens during
  distillation, grouped by error quartile of the control model,
  demonstrating concentrated improvement on challenging plant geometries.}}
  \label{fig:views}

\paragraph{Viewpoint elevation.}
\upd{Camera perspectives form an elevation ladder spanning angles from steep
top-down to steep bottom-up ($\sin\text{elev} = -0.9 + 0.2\,i$).} \upd{Because
every perspective of a plant shares identical 3D ground-truth geometry and
procedural parameters, varying the viewpoint alters sensor input while
keeping target representations constant.}

\upd{Figure~\ref{fig:views}A plots photograph-to-cloud Chamfer error across
camera elevation angles.} \upd{Reconstruction error follows a symmetric U-shaped
distribution: error is lowest for horizontal side-on perspectives and
highest for views looking steeply downward or upward.} \upd{Distillation reduces
reconstruction error by 28\% on average across views while preserving the
overall U-shaped difficulty curve.} \upd{Across 2{,}250 test plants, camera
elevation accounts for $\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$ of per-plant
reconstruction error variance.} \upd{In contrast, plant morphological traits
(leaf count, stem height, slenderness ratio) exhibit negligible correlation
with reconstruction error ($|\rho| \le 0.06$).} \upd{Thus, camera perspective
rather than plant morphology dominates single-image inversion difficulty.}

\paragraph{Training view distribution.}
\upd{We pretrain models under restricted camera elevation bands: top-down views
($i \in \{0,1\}$), horizontal views ($i \in \{4,5\}$), or a single top-down
view ($i=0$), benchmarked against pretraining across all ten views.} \upd{Probes
are evaluated across held-out views $k$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}).} \upd{Restricted models achieve superior performance on their specific training
elevations (e.g., $+0.061$ roll $R^2$ on view 0 for the single-view model),
but experience performance drops on unobserved elevations (falling by
$-0.066$ to $-0.144$ on distant views).} \upd{Models trained on the complete
elevation ladder maintain stable performance across all perspective angles
($R^2 = 0.734\text{--}0.778$).} \upd{Maize gives the same answer without a
per-view probe: its cameras sit at random elevations, so the probe camera is a
random one in every arm. All three restricted arms lose to the all-view arm on
every target (validation $R^2$ for height 0.45, 0.49 and 0.52 against 0.64 for
the top-down, horizontal and single-camera arms; leaf angle 0.82, 0.80 and 0.89
against 0.92; stem radius 0.57, 0.57 and 0.72 against 0.75). The single-camera
arm trains on one tenth of the images but at the probe's elevation distribution
and loses least; the two band arms train on twice as many images inside a band
and lose most, so covering the evaluation elevations matters more than the
number of images.} \upd{Paired bootstraps over plants confirm it: against the
all-view arm the horizontal arm loses all 11 probe targets and the top-down and
single-camera arms lose 10 (height $-0.15$, $-0.19$ and $-0.12$ on validation,
95\,\% intervals of about $\pm 0.02$); no restricted arm wins a target.}
\updtwo{Reconstruction gives the same reading through the point-cloud $F_1$
(protocol of Fig.~\ref{fig:precision_recall}, $\tau = 0.01$, test split). In
sorghum the score is taken on view 0, a top-down camera: the two arms trained
on that elevation reach 0.27 and 0.28 against 0.25 for the all-view arm, while
the horizontal arm, which never saw such a camera, falls to 0.03. In maize the
score is taken on a random camera, and every restricted arm falls below the
all-view arm's 0.31 (horizontal 0.22, top-down 0.19, single camera 0.17).}

\paragraph{Modality value-add.}
\upd{Visual image streams provide the primary source of trait representation
quality.} \upd{On sorghum, adding RGB or depth to point clouds increases height
$R^2$ from $0.895$ to $0.979$ (RGB) and $0.976$ (depth).} \upd{On maize, adding
RGB increases height $R^2$ from $0.171$ to $0.620$.} \upd{Adding depth to
$\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}$ reduces Chamfer distance by 28\% in sorghum
($2.57 \to 1.85 \times 10^{-3}$) and 25\% in maize
($1.61 \to 1.21 \times 10^{-3}$), but provides negligible improvement on
downstream linear probe traits.}

\paragraph{Parameter stream placement dynamics.}
\upd{A critical architectural question is where privileged procedural parameter
tokens should enter a multimodal masked autoencoder.} \upd{When parameter tokens
are visible to the encoder during pretraining, they state the targets
outright; one interpretation is that the network relies on these parameter
tokens as an informational shortcut rather than learning visual features
from sensor patches, so that when parameter tokens are absent at deployment
(where only sensor streams exist), sensor representations are impoverished.}

\upd{In budget-matched controls (Tab.~\ref{tab:token_budget}), restricting
parameter visibility to 0\% (target-only) or 20\% reveals species-specific
dynamics:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Sorghum}: Target-only parameter supervision degrades sensor
  latent probing quality ($R^2$ falls to $0.966$).} \upd{Sorghum parameter
  attributes are tightly coupled (effective rank two); a plausible
  mechanism is gradient competition during multi-task decoding that
  suppresses visual feature learning.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Maize}: Target-only parameter supervision improves trait
  probe scores across all eleven targets ($R^2 = 0.776$ vs.} \upd{$0.633$ on
  height).} \upd{Maize parameters span eleven largely independent dimensions; a
  plausible mechanism is that structured multi-task decoder gradients
  regularize sensor feature representations.}
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:res-metrics}\label{sec:exp-metrics}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_metrics.pdf}
  \caption{\upd{\textbf{Divergence between Chamfer distance and frozen-latent
  probe metrics.} Validation Chamfer distance plotted against height probe
  $R^2$ across twelve model variants.}}
  \label{fig:metrics}\label{fig:metric_divergence}
\end{figure}

\upd{Figure~\ref{fig:metric_divergence} compares validation Chamfer distance
against frozen-latent probe $R^2$ across twelve model variants.} \upd{While the
two metrics show overall rank correlation (Spearman
$\rho = -0.57\text{--}-0.88$), they diverge systematically on key design
choices:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Depth inclusion}: Adding depth maps reduces Chamfer
  distance by 25--28\% while leaving linear probe trait recovery unchanged.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Parameter visibility}: Parameter tokens degrade sensor
  latent probing performance in sorghum while improving surface Chamfer
  distance in maize.}
\end{enumerate}

\upd{Chamfer distance evaluates nearest-neighbor surface proximity
($\min_{q} \|p-q\|_2$).} \upd{On slender crop geometry, placing points near
central stems minimizes Chamfer distance even if entire leaf blades are
omitted.} \upd{In contrast, downstream linear probing evaluates structural
phenotype recovery.} \updtwo{The loss ablation (Supp.~\ref{sec:abl_loss}) confirms the
diagnosis on the training side: QAL alone places points precisely but
covers little of the plant (recall 0.14 at 10\,mm), the Sinkhorn
divergence alone covers the plant but places points poorly (precision
0.14), and their combination, the headline loss, gives the best $F_1$ at
both thresholds.}

\upd{Figure~\ref{fig:precision_recall} plots reconstruction precision, recall,
and $F_1$-score across spatial thresholds $\tau$.} \upd{Across all variants,
precision exceeds recall ($P > R$), confirming that nearest-neighbor
Chamfer loss rewards decoders that cluster points onto central stem
structures while omitting thin outer leaf extremities.}

\subsection{Real-world field validation}
\label{sec:res-field}\label{sec:exp-real}

\updtwo{We test the models on crop rows assembled from real plants of both species. For maize, the rows use the procedural models of 520 real maize plants, whose procedural parameters come from real plants rather than from the generator's distribution. For sorghum, they use 30 scanned real sorghum plants, 2.2--5.3\,m tall. Each row repeats one plant 20 times at 15--25\,cm spacing, in two layouts with the leaf planes parallel and perpendicular to the row. A level camera at half the plant height stops 18 times along the row, each stop centered on one plant, which gives 9{,}360 stops per layout for maize and 540 for sorghum. Each stop provides an image and a point cloud labeled by plant. We convert both to the Regime~III input conventions (a square window, the points the camera sees, and a depth image rendered from them) and score the center plant as the camera sees it. The recipe targets are the maize plant's height and leaf count, since the files carry no per-leaf recipe. For sorghum the files give the total plant height, which the sorghum recipe does not hold (its plant token carries the stem length), so sorghum is scored on its cloud only. The sorghum frames are tall strips framed to the whole plant (7--17$^\circ$ wide, 61--76$^\circ$ high), so the square window is the full frame height with the sides padded. The images and clouds are rendered from the plant models and scans, so this test changes the plants and keeps the sensor synthetic: real illumination and sensor noise are not part of it. The models are those of Sec.~\ref{sec:regime3}, unchanged: the row fine-tune, its isolated-plant control, and the headline model without row fine-tuning. None of them saw these plants.}

\updtwo{On maize, the row fine-tune is the best of the three on both layouts (Tab.~\ref{tab:e10}), with non-overlapping 95\% intervals over plants on Chamfer. It also gives the best height (36\% median relative error, against 41\% for the isolated control and 52\% for the headline model) and the best leaf count (46\% of stops within $\pm1$, against 8\% and 20\%), so training on occluded rows carries over to real plant geometry. With all three streams it beats any single stream (Chamfer 61 against 75 for the cloud alone, 79 for depth and 96 for the image, in $10^{-4}$\,m$^2$ over both layouts). The absolute errors are an order of magnitude above the synthetic test rows, where the row fine-tune reaches a Chamfer of 4.0. Most of this gap is plant size. The real plants are 0.71--2.47\,m tall (median 1.61\,m), 83\% of them are taller than the tallest training plant (1.33\,m), and no model predicts a height above 1.24\,m. On the 87 plants inside the training range, the row fine-tune's median height error is 14\% and its Chamfer 46, against 38\% and 64 on the taller plants. Reading the center plant takes 10.6\,ms per stop on one GPU in batches of 16.}

\updtwo{Sorghum is further from training in every respect: the scanned plants are 2.2--5.3\,m tall while the stems of the training plants reach at most 1.74\,m, and each frame shows a strip about 0.58\,m wide, so the center plant's outer leaves are cut off. No model transfers zero-shot. The Chamfer is 975 for the isolated control, 2{,}145 for the row fine-tune and 2{,}541 for the headline model, against 30.7 for the row fine-tune on synthetic sorghum rows. Every model writes a short, dense plant (Fig.~\ref{fig:e10_real_sorghum}).}

\updtwo{We then fine-tune the row model on 75\% of the real plants of each species and score it on the remaining 25\%, which it never sees. The split is by plant, so both layouts of a plant fall on the same side (maize: 351 training, 39 validation and 130 test plants; sorghum: 19, 3 and 8). The real files supervise the center plant's cloud and its own depth image, rendered from all its points including those the neighbors hide, and, for maize, the plant token's height and leaf count; there is no image loss, because the photograph does not show the hidden parts of the plant. Starting from the row fine-tune, we train for 15 epochs at learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ on one GPU (24 minutes for sorghum, about an hour for maize, evaluation included). On the held-out maize plants the Chamfer falls from 59.5 to 19.7 $\times10^{-4}$\,m$^2$, the median height error from 36\% to 1.6\%, and the share of stops with the leaf count within $\pm1$ rises from 45\% to 70\% (Tab.~\ref{tab:e10}, Fig.~\ref{fig:e10_real}). On the held-out sorghum plants the Chamfer falls from 2{,}113 to 79; with eight test plants this number is indicative only, and the written plants recover the stalk over the full plant height but few of the leaves (Fig.~\ref{fig:e10_real_sorghum}). A few hundred real maize plants, or 19 real sorghum plants, therefore suffice to close most of the gap between the generator and real plant geometry, while the representation and the procedural output stay unchanged.}

\updtwo{The gain holds across inputs, layouts and occlusion (Tab.~\ref{tab:e10_detail}). Every single stream improves: on maize the Chamfer from the image alone falls from 95 to 49, from depth from 77 to 44 and from the cloud from 75 to 47, and on sorghum from depth from 834 to 89; all three streams together remain best. Parallel rows stay easier than perpendicular ones (15.6 against 23.9 after fine-tuning), and across the four quartiles of occlusion the fine-tuned Chamfer stays between 18.5 and 21.6, so the remaining error does not come from the neighbors. Height shows the clearest change (Fig.~\ref{fig:e10_height}). The row fine-tune writes about 1.03\,m for every plant, so its error grows from 15\% inside the training range to 51\% above 2\,m. After fine-tuning the median error is 1.2--2.0\% for plants up to 2.0\,m and 5.9\% above it, where the recipe's height range ends.}\updtwo{ Appendix~\ref{sec:supp-e10} shows 20 more held-out plants, chosen across the range of results.}

\paragraph{Probe protocol for the encoder comparison.}
\updtwo{Table~\ref{tab:baselines} applies the trait probe of Sec.~\ref{sec:field} to every encoder in the same way, with two changes that let the published encoders take part.} \updtwo{First, each plant is represented by the mean of the encoder's output tokens, the one readout that every encoder supports; our models leave their \texttt{[CLS]} token out of this mean.} \updtwo{Second, each encoder receives only the streams it was built for, all taken from view 00 of the plant: DINOv2 the photograph, MultiMAE the photograph and depth frame, Point-MAE the point cloud, EmbodiedMAE all three, and each \method{} encoder the sensor streams it was pretrained on.} \updtwo{The procedural parameters are never an input.} \updtwo{For each encoder and trait, the features of the 10{,}500 training plants are standardized with training statistics only.} \updtwo{A ridge regression is then fitted, with its regularization strength chosen from $\{0.1, 1, 10, 10^2, 10^3, 10^4\}$ by five-fold cross-validation on the training plants.} \updtwo{The table reports $R^2$ on the 2{,}250 validation plants.} \updtwo{The network trained from scratch on the labels goes through the same probe on its frozen features; its own regression head is not used.} \updtwo{The traits are the generator's ground truth for each plant.} \updtwo{In sorghum they are stem length, leaf count, mean leaf branching angle, mean leaf roll and mean leaf length.} \updtwo{In maize they are summed stem internode length, mean leaf angle, mean leaf curvature and stem radius.} \updtwo{The validation and test splits are enriched with extreme plants, with about 1.8 times the training variance in sorghum and 1.5 to 2.6 times in maize.} \updtwo{The $R^2$ values therefore rank the encoders within the table, but they are not comparable with values reported in other work.} \updtwo{Test $R^2$ stays close to validation; for the maize headline model, height reaches $0.747$ on validation and $0.759$ on test.}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\upd{We discuss the core insights, metric divergences, architectural principles,
and limitations established by our experiments.}

\paragraph{Privileged parameter visibility and modality competition.}
\upd{A central design question in multimodal architectures trained alongside
generative models is where privileged structural information should enter
the network.} \upd{The procedural parameter stream $\theta$ is the only modality
that states target phenotypic traits verbatim, yet it is never available at
deployment in the field.} \upd{Across our controlled ablations
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-params}), exposing parameter tokens to the encoder
during pretraining degraded the quality of learned sensor representations
in both sorghum and maize.} \upd{In sorghum, every model arm that saw parameter
tokens in the encoder learned inferior visual features, with the
degradation on the size traits growing with the stream's visibility
(Tab.~\ref{tab:token_budget}).}

\upd{This behavior can be understood through the lenses of shortcut learning,
modality competition~\citep{peng2022ogm}, and negative gradient
transfer~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}.} \upd{When ground-truth parameter tokens are
visible to the transformer encoder, they directly encode target geometry.} \upd{A
plausible account is that the encoder learns a low-cost shortcut through
these parameter tokens, starving the visual (RGB, depth) and point cloud
streams of gradient updates; when parameter tokens are then absent at
inference, the sensor representations prove impoverished.}

\upd{The species-specific control arms (Tab.~\ref{tab:token_budget}) reveal an
additional nuance:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Sorghum}: Keeping parameter tokens strictly as a decoder
  target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) continues to lower sensor latent quality
  ($R^2$ drops from $0.977$ to $0.966$ on height).} \upd{Sorghum morphological
  parameters are tightly coupled (effective rank two); a plausible
  mechanism is gradient competition during multi-task decoding that pulls
  sensor representations toward global plant size, the leading principal
  component of the sorghum latent.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Maize}: Keeping parameters strictly as a decoder target
  ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) or restricting visibility to 20\% improves sensor
  latent probing across all eleven traits ($R^2 = 0.776$ vs.\ $0.633$ on
  height).} \upd{Maize parameters contain 11 independent morphological
  dimensions; supervising them as a generation target provides structured
  multi-task decoder gradients, consistent with a regularizing effect on
  sensor latents without encoder shortcuts.}
\end{itemize}

\upd{These findings yield an architectural design rule for multimodal masked
autoencoders trained on synthetic or procedural data: \emph{privileged
generative parameters should never be largely visible to the encoder during
pretraining}.} \upd{Setting parameter tokens strictly as a decoder reconstruction
target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) or applying heavy gating
($\rho_\theta \le 0.20$) prevents encoder shortcut learning while
preserving generative parameter output capabilities; whether the parameters
help even as a pure reconstruction target depends on the generator.}

\paragraph{Metric divergence on slender geometry.}
\upd{A critical empirical finding of this work is the structural divergence
between nearest-neighbor point cloud metrics (such as Chamfer distance) and
downstream phenotypic trait probing on slender geometry
(Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-metrics}, Fig.~\ref{fig:metric_divergence}).} \upd{Standard
Chamfer distance evaluates surface proximity by computing average
nearest-neighbor point displacements:}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CD}(P, \hat P) = \frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\min_{q\in\hat P}\|p-q\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{|\hat P|}\sum_{q\in\hat P}\min_{p\in P}\|p-q\|_2^2.
  \label{eq:chamfer_def}
\end{equation}
\upd{Because crop organs (such as maize and sorghum leaves) are thin,
high-aspect-ratio surfaces, a predicted point cloud can achieve low Chamfer
distance simply by clustering points onto bulky central stalks while
omitting entire leaf blades.} \upd{In contrast, downstream linear probes fitted
on frozen encoder embeddings directly measure whether latent
representations retain the structural parameter information required for
phenotyping.}

\upd{Throughout our experiments, Chamfer distance and linear probes rank model
variants differently:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Depth stream addition}: Adding metric depth to RGB
  photographs reduces Chamfer distance by 28\% in sorghum and 25\% in
  maize, but yields negligible change in downstream probe $R^2$ scores
  (Tab.~\ref{tab:modality_value_add}).} \upd{Depth ordering provides spatial
  surface proximity for decoders without expanding high-level trait
  information in the encoder.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Data scaling}: Increasing pretraining dataset volume from
  1{,}000 to 10{,}000 plants reduces sorghum Chamfer error by 21\%
  ($3.13 \to 2.48 \times 10^{-3}$), whereas sorghum probe scores saturate
  by 10{,}000 plants (Tab.~\ref{tab:scaling_ablations}).} \item \upd{ \textbf{Partial camera visibility}: Restricting point cloud inputs
  to a single camera perspective increases Chamfer error $2.6\times$ to
  $6.5\times$, whereas refitted linear probes of the fused arms lose at
  most $0.030$ in height $R^2$ (Tab.~\ref{tab:views}).}
\end{enumerate}

\upd{These disagreements demonstrate that Chamfer distance alone is an
insufficient metric for evaluating slender 3D shape reconstruction.} \upd{We
advocate evaluating structural downstream trait probes alongside Chamfer
distance, and incorporating distribution-matching losses (such as Sinkhorn
optimal transport divergence, Eq.~\eqref{eq:point-loss-sinkhorn}) during
training to force decoders to cover full organ geometry rather than
collapsing points onto central stalks.}

\paragraph{Partial coverage, camera viewpoint, and row occlusion.}
\upd{Sensor observations in agricultural field campaigns are inherently partial.} \upd{Our experiments analyze three aspects of partial sensing:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Viewpoint elevation dominance}: Across isolated camera
  perspectives, camera elevation angle dominates single-image
  reconstruction difficulty, accounting for $\eta^2 = 0.24\text{--}0.31$ of
  per-plant reconstruction variance (Fig.~\ref{fig:views}).} \upd{Steep top-down
  and bottom-up perspectives suffer from severe self-occlusion where leaves
  stack vertically along the optical axis, whereas side-on views expose
  full blade silhouettes.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Covariate shift under partial visibility}: While phenotypic
  trait information persists in encoder representations under partial
  surface coverage (refitted 1-camera height $R^2 = 0.960$), directly
  transferring linear probes trained on 100\% complete clouds to partial
  inputs results in performance failure ($R^2 \to -0.26$,
  Fig.~\ref{fig:partial}).} \upd{Partial visibility induces a systematic
  covariate shift in latent feature space.} \upd{Consequently, downstream
  phenotyping readouts deployed in the field must be refit or calibrated on
  sensor-matched partial coverage inputs.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Implicit row occlusion reasoning}: In planted rows,
  neighboring foliage accounts for up to two-thirds of input point clouds
  and pixel areas.} \upd{Standard models trained on isolated plants suffer
  catastrophic failure under row occlusion because decoders attempt to
  incorporate neighboring leaves into target plant reconstructions
  (Tab.~\ref{tab:regime_3}).} \upd{Fine-tuning decoders to predict target main
  plant geometry behind occluding neighbors removes 92\% of
  neighbor-induced occlusion error in maize and 85\% in sorghum, forcing
  the network to perform implicit occlusion reasoning and segment target
  organs from inter-plant contact foliage.}
\end{itemize}

\upd{Importantly, procedural parameter outputs ($\hat\theta$) demonstrate
superior robustness to partial visibility compared to raw point cloud
completions.} \upd{Because the procedural generator $G(\hat\theta)$ acts as an
explicit biological prior, re-rendered procedural meshes generate complete
3D geometry even when input sensor observations capture only a fraction of
the plant surface.}

\paragraph{Capabilities and boundaries of cross-modal distillation.}
\upd{Cross-modal teacher--student distillation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-distill})
converts a multimodal pretraining artifact into a fast, deployable
single-sensor inference model.} \upd{Distilling a full-modality teacher
($\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}+\mathrm{PC}+\theta$) into single-sensor student
networks reduces overall cross-modal generation loss by 30.2\% in sorghum
and 43.5\% in maize (Tab.~\ref{tab:anysubset}).} \upd{Distillation gains are
concentrated on the weakest input modalities (photograph-to-cloud Chamfer
error falls by 61\%) and the most challenging plant geometries
(Fig.~\ref{fig:views}B).} \upd{Furthermore, supervising student models across all
four decoder targets simultaneously outperforms a specialized
parameter-only regression student} \updtwo{(recipe MAE from the point cloud
0.019 against 0.036 and 0.038 for specialists trained at $10^{-4}$ and
$3 \times 10^{-5}$, from the same warm start at 0.040),} \upd{consistent with
auxiliary visual reconstruction tasks acting as regularizing gradients for
parameter regression.}

\upd{However, distillation exhibits clear boundary limits:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Viewpoint robustness}: Distillation lowers absolute
  reconstruction error across all camera views but does not flatten the
  U-shaped elevation difficulty curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:views}A).} \upd{Viewpoint
  robustness cannot be acquired purely through distillation; it requires
  balanced elevation sampling during pretraining data capture
  (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}).} \item \upd{ \textbf{Unobservable parameter instability}: Parameterizations
  submitted to inverse regression must be restricted to traits that field
  sensors can physically resolve.} \upd{In early experimental iterations
  containing unobservable leaf waviness parameters, distillation caused
  these unconstrained parameter heads to develop severe view-dependent
  instability without improving trait skill (skill $\approx 0$).} \upd{Restricting procedural parameter representations to observable
  physiological attributes (insertion heights, blade lengths, widths,
  angles, and rolls) ensures stable cross-modal distillation.}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations and future directions.}
\upd{We acknowledge several limitations in our current study:}
\begin{itemize}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Single-seed ablation budgets}: Controlled ablation
  experiments (Tabs.~\ref{tab:modality_value_add}--\ref{tab:scaling_ablations})
  were executed under a standardized compute budget of 197{,}400 optimizer
  steps using a single seed per arm.} \upd{Run-to-run baseline variance estimated
  from near-duplicate runs is $\approx 2\%$ in Chamfer distance and
  $\le 0.011$ in probe $R^2$; the non-monotone maize scaling differences,
  though significant under the paired bootstrap, could still reflect
  run-to-run variance.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Procedural generator expressivity boundaries}: An inherent
  limitation of inverse procedural modeling is that recovered
  representations are bounded by the expressivity of the underlying
  generator $G$.} \upd{If a physical plant exhibits morphological deformations
  unrepresented in $G$ (such as severe disease lesions, structural
  breakage, or tassel droop, which the generator renders without learnable
  variation), the framework fits the nearest valid procedural parameter
  configuration.} \upd{Expanding procedural generators to support broader
  developmental malformations represents an important avenue for future
  research.} \item \upd{ \textbf{Synthetic renderer regularities}: Synthetic rendering
  pipelines can introduce unintended canonical cues (such as fixed leaf
  plane azimuth alignments) that pose-aware baselines may exploit as
  shortcuts.} \upd{Rigorous evaluation on real-world field captures
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp-real}) remains essential to verify true sim-to-real
  domain transfer.}
\end{itemize}

\upd{By releasing \dataset{} (comprising 30{,}000 synthetic plants across maize
and sorghum with paired multimodal captures, physically simulated row
occlusion, and exact procedural parameter ground truth), we aim to provide
the 3D vision and digital agriculture communities with a benchmark for
evaluating multimodal completion and inverse procedural modeling on
slender, heavily occluded geometry.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\upd{In this work, we presented \method{}, a multimodal masked-autoencoder
framework for recovering editable, biologically interpretable procedural 3D
plant models from fragmentary and occluded field sensor observations.} \upd{By
employing the procedural generator as both the training data engine and the
output representation prior, \method{} converts partial visual, depth, or
point cloud inputs into compact, simulation-ready parameter sets in a
single feed-forward pass taking milliseconds per plant.} \upd{Across extensive
experiments in maize and sorghum, we demonstrated that teacher--student
distillation improves single-sensor parameter regression, while row
fine-tuning eliminates 85\%--92\% of neighbor-induced occlusion error in
realistic crop rows.}

\upd{Our controlled ablations establish two design principles of broader
relevance to multimodal architectures paired with generative models.} \upd{First,
privileged generative structure should not be largely visible to the
encoder during pretraining; doing so degrades sensor latent representations
at deployment, consistent with an informational shortcut through the
privileged tokens.} \upd{Restricting parameter tokens to a decoder-only
reconstruction target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) or applying gated visibility
($\rho_\theta \le 0.20$) prevents shortcut learning while preserving
generative output capabilities.} \upd{Second, nearest-neighbor metrics like
Chamfer distance are structurally insensitive to missing slender organs on
high-aspect-ratio geometry.} \upd{Evaluating structural downstream phenotype
probes provides a more direct measure of recovered structure for
architectural and modality selection on thin 3D shapes.}

\upd{Finally, we introduce \dataset{}, a benchmark pairing multimodal field
captures under simulated row occlusion with exact procedural ground truth
and a real-field evaluation split.} \upd{By unifying generative priors with
self-supervised multimodal representation learning, \method{} connects
field-practical sensor observations with high-throughput, editable 3D plant
analytics.} \updtwo{On crop rows built from real maize and sorghum plants, the row fine-tune transfers zero-shot only for maize, and fine-tuning on 75\% of the real plants cuts the held-out Chamfer by a factor of three for maize and more than twenty for sorghum.}

\section{Detailed Literature Review}
\label{app:litreview}

\upd{This appendix expands the compressed related-work discussion of
Section~\ref{sec:related} across six core bodies of literature on which
\method{} builds and from which it departs.}

\subsection{Procedural plant models and inverse procedural modeling}
\upd{Procedural modeling of plant architecture originated with Lindenmayer
systems (L-systems)~\citep{prusin1990abop} and matured into parametric
generators whose parameters encode physiologically meaningful organ-level
attributes~\citep{floraforge2026,hadadi2025procmaize}.} \upd{Inverting such
models, recovering generative parameters from visual observations, has a
long history for woody trees: Stava et al.\ fit tree generator parameters
via stochastic search~\citep{stava2014ipmtrees}, Talton et al.\ formulated
inverse procedural modeling as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
inference~\citep{talton2011metropolis}, and Guo et al.\ inferred structural
L-systems from 3D point clouds~\citep{guo2020ipmlsys}, with deep generative
variants following~\citep{latentlsys2023}.}

\upd{These methods optimize parameters on a per-instance basis.} \upd{None performs
amortized, single-forward-pass inversion of a procedural plant generator
from noisy, field-degraded sensor data.} \upd{The closest feed-forward precedents
lie outside agriculture: shape-program
induction~\citep{shapeprograms2019}, diffusion-based inverse procedural
content generation from single photographs~\citep{zhao2025dipcg},
text-conditioned CAD generation~\citep{text2cad2024}, and cadrille, which
maps images, point clouds, or text to executable CAD
programs~\citep{kolodiazhnyi2025cadrille}.} \upd{However, these systems operate
on rigid, clean, isolated man-made objects.} \upd{None addresses deformable
slender organs, heavy inter-plant row occlusion, or real-world sensor
noise, and none investigates whether continuous program parameters should
enter the transformer encoder during multimodal pretraining.}

\subsection{Learned parametric recovery of crop plants}
\upd{Recent systems attempt to recover parametric plant models from 3D scans.} \upd{Demeter learns a joint morphology model (topology, articulation, and PCA
surface variations) from soybean point clouds~\citep{demeter2025}, while
Ghrer et al.\ infer grammar-style parameterized crop representations from
3D laser scans~\citep{ghrer2025paramplants}.} \upd{Both systems assume a single,
clean, highly complete point cloud as input, and neither addresses missing
sensor modalities or inter-plant row occlusion.}

\upd{CropCraft recovers procedural crop structure by applying Bayesian
optimization over procedural parameters matched against multi-view NeRF
depth maps~\citep{cropcraft2026}; however, per-scene optimization requires
substantial computation per plant.} \upd{A staged annotation pipeline processes
terrestrial laser scans through vision-language model prompting and fits
procedural NURBS descriptors via differentiable
refinement~\citep{hadadi2026vlmmaize,hadadi2025procmaize,nurbsdiff2022},
requiring $\sim 10$ minutes of computation per isolated plant.} \upd{Tree-D
Fusion reconstructs simulation-ready urban trees from street-level imagery
via diffusion priors~\citep{lee2024treedfusion}, but its
envelope-then-branch pipeline targets woody tree silhouettes rather than
thin, deformable agricultural crop blades.}

\subsection{Point cloud completion and slender geometry}
\upd{Learned point cloud completion has advanced from folding-based
decoders~\citep{yuan2018pcn} to geometry-aware
transformers~\citep{yu2021pointr,yu2023adapointr} and
extrapolation--interpolation architectures~\citep{cai2024einet}.} \upd{Standard
benchmarks (e.g., PCN, ShapeNet-55/34) consist of compact, largely convex
rigid objects and contain no slender-under-occlusion categories.} \upd{Standard
completion algorithms assume missing geometry represents self-occluded
object surfaces without severe inter-object clutter.}

\upd{Plant-specific completion has recently emerged, including amodal
segmentation and geometry completion for occluded
crops~\citep{lee2026occ}.} \upd{However, these methods output unstructured point
clouds rather than editable, biologically interpretable procedural
parameters.} \upd{Curve-aware and thin-structure reconstruction
methods~\citep{curvegaussian2025,qi2026rethinking} address slender
geometry, a regime in which standard Chamfer distance fails due to
structural insensitivity to thin
surfaces~\citep{wu2021dcd,song2026structural}.} \upd{This limitation has
motivated density-aware and information-theoretic distance
variants~\citep{wu2021dcd,lin2023infocd}.} \upd{In this work, we systematically
compare surface geometric metrics against downstream linear trait probes on
frozen encoder latents, demonstrating that the two metrics rank
architectural choices differently on slender plant geometry.}

\subsection{3D plant phenotyping}
\upd{Organ-level 3D phenotyping datasets, including Pheno4D's annotated maize
and tomato temporal sequences~\citep{schunck2021pheno4d},
Crops3D~\citep{crops3d2024}, and field-scale maize scans carrying
procedural reference fits~\citep{maizefield3d2025}, support trait
extraction using deep point cloud
architectures~\citep{qi2017pointnet,qi2017pointnetpp}.} \upd{Furthermore,
field-based radiance-field capture is increasingly
viable~\citep{arshad2024nerf_field}.} \upd{However, no existing benchmark
combines real-field captures, slender plant geometry, physically simulated
inter-plant row occlusion, and exact procedural ground-truth parameter
targets across multiple crop species.} \upd{\dataset{} is designed to fill this
gap.}

\subsection{Multimodal masked pretraining and distillation}
\upd{Masked autoencoding~\citep{he2022mae} has been extended to multimodal
domains: MultiMAE over RGB, depth, and semantic
segmentation~\citep{bachmann2022multimae}; 4M and 4M-21 over tokenized
visual, structural, and textual
modalities~\citep{mizrahi2023fourm,bachmann2024fourm21}; Point-MAE over 3D
point clouds~\citep{pang2022pointmae}; and EmbodiedMAE over RGB, depth, and
point clouds for robotic manipulation~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}.}

\upd{Maintaining robustness under missing input modalities is an active area of
research~\citep{wang2024robustmissing}.} \upd{Cross-modal feature distillation
from multi-sensor teacher models to single-sensor student models is
established for discriminative tasks~\citep{hinton2015distillation}.} \upd{We
extend cross-modal distillation to a \emph{generative} setting for
structured parameter regression, encoding continuous procedural parameters
as numerical tokens via xVal-style embedding~\citep{golkar2023xval}.} \upd{Our
finding that exposing ground-truth parameter tokens to the encoder degrades
visual representation quality connects to modality
competition~\citep{peng2022ogm} and gradient
interference~\citep{yu2020pcgrad,chen2018gradnorm}, establishing a concrete
design rule for privileged generative modalities in multimodal pretraining.}

\subsection{Synthetic-data pretraining and sim-to-real transfer}
\upd{Pretraining deep representations on synthetic data is established across
domains ranging from fractal geometry~\citep{kataoka2020fractaldb} to
procedural 3D environments~\citep{raistrick2023infinigen,greff2022kubric},
with domain randomization closing the sim-to-real
gap~\citep{tobin2017domainrand}.} \upd{The utility of synthetic data is known to
depend heavily on downstream task alignment~\citep{fan2023intriguing}, and
model self-consumption presents documented stability
risks~\citep{alemohammad2023mad}.}

\upd{Our setting represents an ideal use-case for synthetic pretraining: the
deployment target is the exact parameter space of the procedural generator,
the generator spans the continuous morphological variation of target crop
species, and the framework is validated against high-density real-world
field captures~\citep{maizefield3d2025}.}

\subsection{Token-budget control arms}
\upd{In default four-stream pretraining, sensor streams share a total visible
budget of 122--123 tokens with parameter tokens (yielding 107.4 visible
sensor tokens vs.\ 117.0 in three-stream models, with parameter tokens
$\sim 58\%$ visible).} \upd{Control arms decouple parameter tokens from the
sensor token allocation, restoring a full 117.0 visible sensor token
allocation across three parameter visibility levels: 20\%, 60\%, and 0\%
(target-only).}

\upd{In sorghum, restoring sensor token budgets does not recover three-stream
probing performance: height $R^2$ remains $0.959\text{--}0.966$ (vs.\
$0.977$), and leaf roll $R^2$ remains $0.702\text{--}0.771$ (vs.\
$0.781$).} \upd{In maize, 20\% parameter visibility and target-only parameter
supervision outperform both the shared-budget four-stream model and
three-stream $\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}$ across all eleven traits
on both splits.}

\subsection{Parameters as a decoder reconstruction target only}
\upd{Restricting procedural parameters strictly to a decoder reconstruction
target ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$, never encoded as input) still degrades sensor
representations in sorghum relative to omitting the stream: height $R^2$
drops by $-0.011$, leaf roll by $-0.07$, mean leaf length by $-0.10$, and
leaf width by $-0.15$ on validation.} \upd{The learned latent space becomes
heavily dominated by global plant scale: the first principal component
accounts for 44\% of total latent variance and correlates strongly with
leaf count ($|r| = 0.95$), while the participation ratio drops to 4.3
(vs.\ 6.7 for $\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}$).} \upd{Conversely, in maize,
target-only parameter supervision ($\rho_\theta = 1.0$) yields the
top-performing four-stream model, outperforming
$\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}$ across all eleven traits.}

\subsection{Distillation dynamics per source modality}
\upd{Distillation from the full-modality teacher reduces overall cross-modal
generation loss by 30.2\% in sorghum and 43.5\% in maize (evaluating
epoch-0 warm-start initialization against epoch-100 distilled students).} \upd{Gains are concentrated on the weakest input modalities:
point-cloud-to-cloud Chamfer error in sorghum drops from
$5.48 \times 10^{-3}$ to $1.39 \times 10^{-3}$, parameter-only-to-cloud
Chamfer drops from $22.7 \times 10^{-3}$ to $6.83 \times 10^{-3}$, and
photograph-to-cloud Chamfer in maize falls from $11.3 \times 10^{-3}$ to
$0.77 \times 10^{-3}$.} \upd{Parameter regression MAE from point clouds alone is
halved in sorghum ($0.040 \to 0.020$).} \updtwo{The parameter-only specialist
was retrained on the final data from the same headline warm start with the
first-release recipe (point-cloud source, half of its tokens hidden, recipe
target only, 100 epochs), once at the generalist's learning rate of $10^{-4}$
and once at the first-release rate of $3 \times 10^{-5}$. Scored on the full
validation split with the point cloud fully visible, the specialists reach a
recipe MAE of 0.036 and 0.038 (warm start 0.040), the generalist 0.019: the
student that also generates the photograph, the depth frame and the cloud
recovers the recipe better than the student trained on the recipe alone, at
either learning rate.}

\subsection{Label-free view invariance across camera perspectives}
\upd{Because ten camera perspectives per plant share identical ground-truth
procedural parameters, prediction variance across perspectives provides a
label-free metric of view invariance.} \upd{Teacher--student distillation reduces
both absolute parameter error and prediction spread across camera views by
$\sim 50\%$ on fields with skill (insertion height error reduced by 59\%,
branching angle error reduced by 60\%).} \upd{Zero-skill fields instead become
more view-dependent under distillation, as the head starts to track image
noise; the final generator drops those fields.}

\subsection{Frozen-encoder baselines and pose cues}
\upd{On sorghum, three-stream \method{} ($\mathrm{PC}+\mathrm{RGB}+\mathrm{D}$)
outperforms all published self-supervised visual encoders
(DINOv2~\citep{oquab2024dinov2}, MultiMAE~\citep{bachmann2022multimae},
Point-MAE~\citep{pang2022pointmae}, and
EmbodiedMAE-B~\citep{dong2025embodiedmae}) across all traits.} \upd{Paired over
plants against EmbodiedMAE-B, \method{} wins 8 of 9 targets (height $R^2$
$+0.005$, roll $+0.04$, leaf length $+0.08$, leaf width $+0.09$).} \upd{On maize,
four-stream \method{} outperforms EmbodiedMAE-B on 10 of 11 traits.}

\upd{Evaluating Point-MAE with full 3D camera pose matrices yields inflated
performance on synthetic maize due to a renderer artifact: synthetic maize
scenes align leaf planes along a canonical world plane, enabling full pose
matrices to leak plant azimuth orientation.} \upd{Evaluating Point-MAE with
gravity-aligned camera poses (realistic field calibration) reduces its
height $R^2$ from $0.720$ to $0.588$, while pose-free \method{} wins 5 of 9
decided traits.}

\subsection{Latent space structure analysis (E9)}
\upd{Frozen encoder \texttt{[CLS]} embedding spaces form continuous, smooth
manifolds without discrete clustering, as expected for continuous synthetic
generators (best silhouette coefficient $0.15\text{--}0.24$ at $k=2$,
adjusted Rand index $\approx 0.0$ against shape deciles, t-SNE
trustworthiness $0.95\text{--}0.99$).} \upd{The latent effective participation
ratio is low: $4.4\text{--}6.3$ (out of 768 dimensions) in sorghum and
$6.7\text{--}11.3$ in maize.} \upd{Explaining 90\% of latent variance requires
14--15 principal components in sorghum and 18--19 in maize.} \upd{In sorghum, the
leading principal component aligns with global plant scale (correlation
with leaf count $|r| = 0.73\text{--}0.94$).}


\subsection{Source of Occlusion}
\label{sec:abl_occ}

\updtwo{Rendering realistic occluded scenes is expensive, so we ask whether cheaper, synthetic occlusion can replace it
during training. We compare four sources of occlusion (\cref{fig:occ_sources}) and test every model on the same
rendered occluded scenes.}

  \centering

  \caption{\updtwo{The four training sources of occlusion, shown for the same maize target plant. Columns: the target
  plant alone; synthetic neighbours (the 3D point clouds of other plants placed beside the target and rendered
  into the image and depth map); B\'ezier leaves (leaf-shaped occluders drawn in the image and depth map and

\paragraph{\updtwo{Setup.}}
\updtwo{Each rendered maize row shows a target plant with two neighbouring plants 0.15\,m to either side, captured by a
level camera 1\,m away; the reconstruction target is the point cloud of the target plant alone, rendered from
the same camera. All models are EmbodiedMAE-4M (base), start from the same checkpoint pretrained on single
plants, and are trained for 200 epochs with a cosine schedule, learning rate $1.5\times10^{-4}$, a global batch
of 32, 80\% random token masking and the point-cloud loss QAL $+\,0.5\times$Sinkhorn. They differ only in the
occlusion added to the training inputs. \emph{Plant alone}: no occlusion. \emph{Synthetic neighbours}: the
point clouds of two other plants from the batch are placed beside the target in 3D, 0.15--0.35\,m away, and
rendered into the image and depth map with a z-buffer. \emph{B\'ezier leaves}: three to eight leaf-shaped
B\'ezier curves are drawn into the image and depth map, and their visible pixels are lifted to 3D points
through the camera. \emph{Rendered row}: the rendered scene itself. Half of the training samples receive the
occlusion and half show the target plant alone, and the procedural parameters are hidden on half of the steps.}

\paragraph{\updtwo{Metrics.}}
\updtwo{All models are tested on the rendered rows: 2{,}250 test plants, one fixed view each, with the occluded scene
as input and no procedural parameters. Precision is the fraction of predicted points within 10\,mm of the
target plant, recall the fraction of target points with a prediction within 10\,mm, and F1 their harmonic
mean, averaged over plants.}

\subsection{Mask Ratio During Post-Training}
\label{sec:abl_mask}

\updtwo{We study how much of the input should be masked when the model is trained to reconstruct a plant that is
partly hidden by its neighbours.}

\paragraph{\updtwo{Setup.}}
\updtwo{We use rendered sorghum rows: each scene shows a target plant with two neighbouring plants 0.15\,m to either
side, captured by a level camera 2.5\,m away. The input is the RGB image, depth map and point cloud of the

are hidden, split across the streams by a Dirichlet draw; $m=0$ keeps every token visible. Half of the
training samples show the occluded scene and half the target plant alone, and the procedural parameters are
hidden on half of the steps. Since every variant starts from a model trained at $m=0.8$, this ablation
measures the mask ratio used for fine-tuning on occluded scenes.}

\subsection{Point-Cloud Loss}
\label{sec:abl_loss}

\updtwo{We compare point-cloud losses for reconstructing single sorghum plants.}

\paragraph{\updtwo{Losses.}}
\updtwo{Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the predicted point cloud and $\mathcal{T}$ the target cloud, and let}
\begin{equation}
  \updtwo{
  d(x,\mathcal{S})=\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\lVert x-s\rVert_2
  =\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}(x_k-s_k)^2}
  }
  \label{eq:dist}
\end{equation}
\updtwo{be the Euclidean (not squared) distance from a point $x$ to the nearest point of a cloud $\mathcal{S}$. The
quality-aware loss (QAL) weights every nearest-neighbour distance by how far it is from a tolerance $\tau$:}
\begin{equation}
  \updtwo{
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QAL}} ={}& \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} w\big(d(p,\mathcal{T})\big)\,d(p,\mathcal{T})\\
      &+ \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}} w\big(d(t,\mathcal{P})\big)\,d(t,\mathcal{P}),
  \end{aligned}
  }
  \label{eq:qal}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \updtwo{
  w(d) = \sigma\big(\alpha\,(d-\tau)\big),
  }
  \label{eq:qal_w}
\end{equation}
\updtwo{where $\sigma$ is the logistic function. Points within $\tau$ of the other cloud get a weight below $1/2$
(down to $\sigma(-\alpha\tau)$ at $d=0$), while points far beyond $\tau$ get a weight close to 1, so training
concentrates on the badly placed points, such as missing leaf tips; $\alpha$ sets how sharply the weight switches
at $\tau$. We use
$\alpha=100$ and $\tau=10$\,mm (the clouds are normalised per plant, so $\tau$ is 0.01 of the plant radius, \ie
10\,mm for a plant of median size). \emph{Sinkhorn} is the debiased Sinkhorn divergence $S_\varepsilon(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T})$ ($p=2$,
blur 0.02) on 1{,}024 sampled points, and \emph{QAL\,+\,Sinkhorn} is
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QAL}}+\lambda\,S_\varepsilon(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{T})$ with $\lambda=0.5$. Each point-cloud
loss enters the total loss with weight 1.}

\paragraph{\updtwo{Single plants.}}
\updtwo{Models are trained from scratch on Sorghum\_15K (10{,}500 training plants, one random view per plant per epoch,
each plant rendered alone) for 200 epochs (65.8k steps) with a cosine schedule, learning rate
$1.5\times10^{-4}$, a global batch of 32 and 80\% token masking, and differ only in the point-cloud loss. They
are tested on all 2{,}250 test plants with the plant alone as input.}

\paragraph{\updtwo{Metrics.}}
\updtwo{Precision is the fraction of predicted points within a threshold of the target plant, recall the fraction of
target points with a prediction within the threshold, and F1 their harmonic mean, averaged over plants. We write
F1@$x$, Prec.@$x$ and Rec.@$x$ for a threshold of $x$\,mm.}

\subsection{Mixed Masking}
\label{sec:abl_mixed}

\updtwo{When the training input is occluded, the masking can target the occluder. \emph{Structured} masking hides the
tokens that show an occluder first (an image patch that is at least 30\% occluder pixels, or a point-cloud token
whose centre is an occluder point) and fills the rest of the masking budget at random. \emph{Mixed} masking flips
one coin per occluded training scene: with probability 0.5 the scene is masked structured, otherwise uniformly at
random (\cref{fig:mixed}). We compare mixed masking with uniform \emph{random} masking. Validation and test always
use random masking.}

\updtwo{All models are EmbodiedMAE-4M (base) trained for 200 epochs with a cosine schedule, a global batch of 32, 80\% token

\section{Dataset Generation Details}
\label{app:data}

\upd{\dataset{} contains four synthetic sets: isolated single-plant captures of
maize and sorghum, alongside simulated \emph{row scenes} captured by a
level ground rover.} \upd{All images are rendered at $1024 \times 1024$
resolution with a $40^\circ$ vertical field of view
($f_x = f_y = 512 / \tan 20^\circ = 1406.7$\,px, principal point at image
center) in OpenGL camera coordinates ($+y$ up, camera looking along $-z$).}

\subsection{Procedural crop generation}
\paragraph{Maize procedural generation.} Each plant $i$ of $15{,}000$ is
\upd{generated from seed $i$ using a procedural maize generator.} \upd{Morphological
traits are sampled from Gaussian distributions: plant height
$0.832 \pm 0.104$\,m, leaf count $12.35 \pm 2.57$, base leaf length
$0.448 \pm 0.057$\,m, base leaf width $0.066 \pm 0.010$\,m, and base leaf
insertion angle $43.98^\circ \pm 10.13^\circ$.} \upd{Attributes are coupled
through four latent traits (vigor, slenderness, posture, texture) with
correlation strength 0.735, canopy position curves (bottom, middle, top),
and per-leaf spatial jitter (scale 0.687).} \upd{Plant heights span 0.62--1.44\,m
(median 1.01\,m).} \upd{Parameter records store plant height, leaf count, and
per-leaf stem insertion height, length, width, azimuth, insertion angle,
and droopiness.} \upd{Per-leaf curvature is generated by constructing midrib
B-splines and integrating bending curvature along leaf blades using
10-node Gauss--Legendre quadrature.}

\paragraph{Sorghum procedural generation.} Each plant $i$ of $15{,}000$ is
\upd{generated from seed $i$ using the EvoEngine sorghum generator.} \upd{Heading
angle is sampled uniformly in $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$, stem tilt
$0^\circ \pm 1.5^\circ$, leaf count $20 \pm 2$ (clamped to $[2, 128]$), and
internode spacing $0.04 \pm 0.03$\,m.} \upd{Stem length is evaluated as
$\text{internode} \times n_{\text{leaves}} / (1 - s_0)$, where $s_0$
represents the first leaf insertion point.} \upd{Continuous curves over relative
height $t \in [0, 1]$ determine leaf insertion height, blade length, width,
roll ($\text{roll}_k = (k \bmod 2) \cdot 180^\circ + \text{roll}(t)$),
branching angle, and bending curvature.} \upd{Sorghum plants contain 4--35 leaves
(mean 19.5) on stems ranging from 0.19\,m to 1.74\,m.}

\subsection{Dataset partitioning}
\upd{Both species are partitioned into 10{,}500 training, 2{,}250 validation,
and 2{,}250 test plants (70\% / 15\% / 15\%).}
\begin{itemize}
\item \upd{ \textbf{Maize partitioning}: 19 scalar morphological features are
  robustly standardized using median absolute deviation
  ($z = (x - \mathrm{median}) / (1.4826 \cdot \mathrm{MAD})$).} \upd{Each plant
  is scored by Mahalanobis distance
  $\sqrt{z^\top (\Sigma + 10^{-6}I)^{+} z}$.} \upd{The most extreme plants are
  dealt alternately to test and validation splits to form an extreme tail
  split (train scores 1.56--4.60, validation 4.60--23.3, test 4.60--32.2).} \item \upd{ \textbf{Sorghum partitioning}: Plants are partitioned based on
  Mahalanobis distance across six core traits: leaf count, stem length,
  mean leaf length, maximum leaf length, mean leaf roll, and leaf roll
  standard deviation.} \upd{Sampling probabilities scale continuously with
  extremeness rank, rising from 0.3\% in the least extreme 5\% of plants to
  70\% in the most extreme 5\% (median rank 0.75 on validation and test,
  0.37 on train).} \upd{The rule is: each plant's extremeness is its Mahalanobis
  distance from the centroid of the six standardized traits, ranked to a
  percentile $r\in[0,1]$; a pool of 30\,\% of the plants is drawn without
  replacement with probability proportional to $(r+0.02)^2$ (seed 42), shuffled,
  and split equally into test and validation, and the rest is the training split.
  Re-running this procedure (\texttt{data\_split/make\_split.py}) reproduces
  the released assignment for all 15{,}000 plants.}
\end{itemize}
\upd{Figure~\ref{fig:dataset_stats} shows the resulting split densities, the
trait structure of the two generators and the occlusion the row scenes
produce.}

\subsection{Isolated-plant sensor rendering}
\upd{Off-screen rendering is executed via \texttt{pyrender}.} \upd{Cameras target the
plant bounding-box center $\mathbf{c}$ with world up $(0,1,0)$.} \upd{Lighting
consists of 0.15 ambient illumination plus three white directional lights
directed at $\mathbf{c}$ from $(1,1,1)$, $(-1,0.5,-0.5)$, and $(0,1,0)$
(intensities 4.0, 2.0, 1.5) against a neutral gray background (0.5).} \upd{Depth
$z \in [n, f]$ is packed losslessly into 32-bit RGBA PNG files:}
\begin{equation}
  v = \left\lfloor \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{z-n}{f-n}, 0, 1\right) (2^{32}-1) \right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
\upd{Pixels $(u,v)$ with depth $z > 0$ back-project to 3D camera coordinates:}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{cam}} = \left( \frac{u - c_x}{f_x} z,\; -\frac{v - c_y}{f_y} z,\; -z \right).
  \label{eq:supp-backproject}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Maize captures.} Ten camera directions per plant are sampled
\upd{uniformly on the sphere at distance $1.05\,r / \sin 20^\circ$
($r$ = bounding-box half-diagonal).} \upd{A fourth directional light (1.5) is
mounted to the camera.} \upd{Outputs include RGB images, RGBA images with cleared
backgrounds, depth maps, camera poses, parameter records, and
8{,}192-point clouds back-projected from view depth maps.}

\paragraph{Sorghum captures.} Ten camera directions form a fixed Fibonacci
\upd{sphere rig at 2.5\,m distance ($y_i = 1 - 2(i+0.5)/10$, elevation angles
$-64^\circ$ to $+64^\circ$).} \upd{Outputs include RGB images, depth maps, camera
poses, parameter records, and complete occlusion-free 8{,}192-point clouds
expressed in the camera frame.}

\subsection{Simulated crop row rendering}
\paragraph{Row composition.} Within a split, row $i$ places target main
\upd{plant $L_i$ between right neighbor $L_{i+1}$ and left neighbor $L_{i+2}$ at
stem spacing $d = 0.15$\,m along the $x$-axis.} \upd{Rows are rendered across ten
orientation angles $\theta \in \{0^\circ, 10^\circ, \dots, 90^\circ\}$ with
random neighbor offsets $\delta \sim U(-15^\circ, 15^\circ)$.} \upd{Sorghum
neighbors are rotated so their stem axes run parallel to the main stem.}

\paragraph{Physical contact solver.} Inter-plant leaf collisions are
\upd{resolved using an articulated contact solver.} \upd{Leaf blades are modeled as
chains of 8\,cm segments connected by joints that bend along circular arcs,
up/down and sideways.} \upd{Stems, sheaths (within 2.5\,cm of stem axes), and
maize tassels remain rigid.} \upd{Upon collision, joints rotate by the minimum
clearing angle required to eliminate triangle--triangle intersection.} \upd{Neighbors bend first; main plant leaves bend only when neighbors reach
rotation limits (Tab.~\ref{tab:supp-stages}).} \upd{When no valid layout exists
under default limits, limits loosen in stages, and stem spacing widens in
5\,cm steps (refined to 1\,cm); the maximum spacing used in maize is
0.33\,m.}

\paragraph{Independent mesh intersection validation.}
\upd{Solved row layouts are verified by an independent validation suite that
shares no code with the solver: it executes exact triangle--triangle
intersection checks between adjacent plant meshes at their rendered
positions, and a row passes only if no triangle of one plant intersects a
triangle of another.} \upd{The check found no inter-plant mesh intersections on
the rows it was run on; which rows those were is not recorded, and the bent
meshes are not stored with the renders, so it cannot be repeated on the released
data.} \upd{The solver's own record covers every row: across all 300{,}000 row
orientations (15{,}000 rows $\times$ 10 yaws per species), none is marked failed,
