Editable, biologically interpretable 3D models of fieldgrown crops support high-throughput phenotyping, in silico breeding trials, and agronomic digital twins, but recovering such models from field sensor data remains difficult: plant organs are slender, neighboring plants occlude one another, and a field campaign rarely yields more
than a single photograph, a noisy depth frame, or a partial
point cloud per plant. Slender geometry under occlusion
is a regime in which learned 3D completion and Chamferbased evaluation both fail. In this work, we present C ROP C ODE, a multimodal masked-autoencoder framework that
recovers the complete procedural representation of a plant,
the generator parameters encoding leaf count, insertion
heights, blade angles, and surface geometry, from any subset of RGB, depth, and point-cloud input in a single forward pass. The model is pretrained on 10K–100K procedurally generated maize and sorghum plants rendered with
physically simulated row occlusion, and a full-modality
teacher is then distilled into single-sensor students. Distillation improves cross-modal generation by 19% on average and parameter recovery from a point cloud alone by
44%. Controlled ablations, replicated across both species,
show that the procedural parameters are valuable prediction targets but do not help as a pretraining modality: exposing them to the encoder degrades phenotype recovery
on every target we measure. We further show that Chamfer distance and linear probes on the frozen representation rank input modalities differently on slender geometry,
and we adopt the probe as the deciding metric. On real
field scans with independently reconstructed ground truth,
C ROP C ODE matches/approaches the accuracy of a staged
annotation pipeline and reduces the cost of procedural reconstruction from hours of manual specification, or minutes
of pipeline computation, to milliseconds per plant. We release F IELD P ROC, the N-plant, two-species benchmark behind these experiments, which pairs multimodal field captures under simulated row occlusion with exact procedural
ground truth. These results establish a practical route from
fragmentary field sensing to simulation-ready 3D crop field

1. Introduction

041

Accurate three-dimensional models of individual crop
plants enable high-throughput phenotyping of morphological traits such as leaf angle, internode length, and canopy architecture, and they underpin emerging agricultural digital
twins that run breeding trials in silico [19, 25]. The representation best suited to these uses is procedural: a compact
set of biologically interpretable parameters that a generator
expands into complete, editable, simulation-ready geometry [20, 35]. A procedural plant occupies a few kilobytes
where a laser scan occupies megabytes, every parameter
corresponds to a trait an agronomist works with, and the
model can be edited, re-rendered, and perturbed in ways
that unstructured geometry does not support. Field crops
also instantiate a problem of general interest in 3D computer vision: recovering a parametric model of a thin, heavily occluded object from partial multimodal observations.

Yet recovering procedural structure from field-acquired
sensor data remains a significant challenge, and every existing route presupposes a rich, clean observation. Manual
parameter specification takes hours per plant [20]. Staged
reconstruction pipelines automate the process; a recent system annotates laser scans with a vision-language model
and fits a procedural descriptor to 5.4 mm median accuracy, but it requires a complete scan of an isolated plant
and roughly ten minutes of computation per plant [17]. Perscene optimization methods search generator parameters
against multi-view imagery [52], and recent learned models
infer parametric plants directly, but only from a clean, complete point cloud [8, 13]. A phenotyping campaign supplies
the opposite: thousands of plants per day, each observed by
whichever sensor was practical: a camera, a depth frame, or
a partial scan.

The framework also answers a design question that any
model with access to generative structure must face: where
should the parameter stream enter? Our controlled ablations give a precise answer: the parameters are valuable prediction targets, and a liability as a representation-learning
modality. With token budgets matched, adding the parameter stream to the encoder degrades the frozen representation on every sorghum phenotype target and on most
maize targets; the result survives budget-restoring control
arms, replicates across both species, and holds even when
the parameters serve only as a reconstruction target for
the decoder. Supervising parameters as a generation target is nevertheless what makes the distilled generalist outperform a parameter-only specialist at parameter regression
itself. One interpretation, consistent with modality competition [34] and negative transfer [48], is that a stream
that already contains the answer suppresses learning in the
streams that must supply it at test time.
Evaluating these models exposed a metric disagreement
with consequences beyond plants. Chamfer distance ranks
depth as the most valuable input modality (−32%), whereas
a linear probe on the frozen representation, read out on the
phenotypic traits the application needs, ranks depth within
noise and identifies RGB as the carrier of trait information. On slender geometry the two metrics measure different quantities, surface proximity and recovered structure,
and we adopt the probe as the deciding metric for modality
decisions [6, 47].
The claim of this work is that fragmentary field sensing suffices to recover a complete, interpretable procedural plant, provided the generator supplies both the training
data and the output representation, and its parameters are
withheld from the encoder. We validate this claim on real
field scans of maize and sorghum with independently reconstructed ground truth [17, 25], including rows in which
neighbors occlude the target. one sentence of headline realdata results when available. The key contributions of this
work are:
1. Task and framework. The first formulation and system,
to our knowledge, for recovering procedural plant representations of slender, row-occluded field crops from
any subset of RGB, depth, and point-cloud input in one
amortized forward pass, with full-modal to single-sensor
distillation (+19.1% generation, −43.8% parameter error from point clouds alone).
2. A design rule for structured modalities. Controlled,
budget-matched, two-species evidence that generator parameters are valuable prediction targets, while treating
them as an auxiliary representation-learning modality
does not improve, and can degrade, the sensor representation; we expect this guidance to transfer to any multimodal model with access to privileged generative structure.

A second, geometric barrier compounds the practical
one. Crop plants are slender: a maize blade is centimeters wide, millimeters thick, and up to a meter long. In a
planted row, each plant is further occluded by its neighbors,
whose leaves interleave with the target; in our field simulations, roughly a third of the points inside a plant’s bounding
region belong to neighboring plants. This regime violates
the assumptions of learned 3D completion: the standard
benchmarks contain compact, largely convex objects and
no slender-under-occlusion category [49, 51], and Chamfer distance, the default metric, is structurally insensitive to
thin sheets, so it can reward reconstructions that miss entire
blades [42, 47]. Occlusion-aware completion of thin plant
organs has begun to address the input side of this regime,
but its output is a completed point cloud rather than structure [27]. To our knowledge, no prior method recovers
a procedural, re-renderable representation of slender crop
organs from a single field-practical observation (a photograph, a depth frame, or a partial point cloud) under rowneighbor occlusion: methods that infer plant parameters require clean, complete scans of isolated plants [8, 13], and
methods that tolerate field occlusion complete unstructured
geometry instead [27].
In this work, we present C ROP C ODE, a framework that
makes this inverse problem tractable by using the procedural generator itself as the prior. We pretrain a four-stream
masked autoencoder [3, 10, 21, 31] over RGB, depth, point
clouds, and tokenized procedural parameters of 10K–100K
generated maize and sorghum plants, rendered in simulated
rows in which neighboring leaves bend on contact. Because
random modality subsets are masked during pretraining, a
single model learns to complete any subset at inference. A
full-modality teacher is then distilled [22] into the singlesensor students that deployment requires. Distillation improves cross-modal generation by 19.1% on average, by
27.5% when conditioning on a point cloud alone, and parameter recovery from that point cloud by 43.8%.

2. Related Work

181
182

Recovering procedural plants. Inverse procedural modeling has a long history for woody plants: parameters of tree
generators have been recovered by stochastic search [43],
by Markov chain Monte Carlo inference [44], and by inferring L-systems directly from point clouds [16, 28]. These
methods optimize or infer per instance. Recent systems
address crops specifically. Demeter learns a parametric
morphology model from soybean point clouds [8], Ghrer
et al. infer grammar-style plant representations from 3D
scans [13], CropCraft fits a procedural crop model by
Bayesian optimization against multi-view imagery [52], and
a recent staged pipeline reconstructs procedural maize from
terrestrial laser scans through vision-language-model annotation and differentiable NURBS refinement [9, 17]. Each
of these methods presupposes a single rich observation, a
clean and complete point cloud or a multi-view capture of
an isolated plant, and none addresses missing modalities or
inter-plant occlusion.

Multimodal masked pretraining. Masked autoencoding [21] extends to multiple modalities in MultiMAE [3], to
any-to-any generation over tokenized modalities in 4M [4,
31], to point clouds in Point-MAE [33], and to combined
RGB, depth, and point cloud inputs in EmbodiedMAE,
from which our architecture derives [10]. Robustness to
missing modalities is a recognized concern [40], and distillation from multi-sensor teachers to single-sensor students
is established for discriminative tasks [22]. We extend this
machinery in two ways: we add a structured generative
modality, encoded with continuous number tokens [14], and
we distill a generative full-modality teacher for structured
parameter regression.

Learning from procedural data. Pretraining on purely
synthetic data is viable, from abstract fractals [23] to procedurally generated scenes [15, 39], with domain randomization bridging the gap to real data [46] and with the
value of synthetic data known to depend on the downstream
task [11]. Our setting is the favorable case for this strategy:
the procedural generator defines the output representation,
spans the morphology of the target crop, and is paired with
real field evaluation data [25]. Our results add one caveat
to the recipe: the generator’s parameters belong in the data
and in the targets, and should be withheld from the encoder
(Sec. 4.6).

Programs from observations. Outside agriculture, decoding parametric programs from observations is an established pattern: neural shape programs [45], diffusionbased inverse procedural content generation from a single image [53], text-conditioned CAD generation [24], and
cadrille, which maps images, point clouds, or text to executable CAD models [26]. These systems operate on rigid
objects observed cleanly and in isolation. Our setting differs in the geometry (deformable, slender organs), in the input (noisy, occluded field sensors), and in a design question
that none of them raises: whether the program parameters
should be visible to the encoder at all.

A.1. Procedural plant models and inverse procedural modeling

557
558
559
560
561

573
574
575
576

Procedural modeling of plants begins with L-systems [35]
and matured into generators whose parameters are organlevel, biologically meaningful quantities [19, 20]. Inverting
such models, that is, recovering their parameters from observations, has a long history for woody species: Stava et
al. fit tree parameters by stochastic search [43], Talton et al.
formulated procedural inversion as MCMC inference [44],
and Guo et al. inferred L-systems directly from point
clouds [16], with neural variants following [28]. These
methods optimize or infer per instance; amortized, feedforward inversion of a plant generator from field-degraded
input is the gap this paper fills. The closest amortized precedents sit outside agriculture: shape-program induction [45],
diffusion-based inverse procedural content generation from
a single image [53], and cadrille, which maps images, point
clouds, or text to executable CAD programs [24, 26]. All
operate on rigid, isolated, clean-input objects; none confronts deformable slender organs, inter-plant occlusion, or
real-sensor noise, and none studies whether the program parameters should participate in the encoder.

Learned completion runs from PCN [51] through transformer approaches [49, 50] to recent extrapolation–
interpolation designs [5], trained and evaluated on benchmarks of compact rigid objects. Neither PCN’s eight
categories nor ShapeNet-55/34 contains a slender-underocclusion class, and completion systems assume the missing geometry is self-occluded surface, absent competing
clutter from other objects inside the crop region. Plantspecific completion is emerging, with amodal segmentation
plus completion for occluded plants [27], but it outputs geometry rather than structure or parameters. Curve-aware
and thin-structure reconstruction [12, 38] shows growing
awareness of the slender regime, on which the standard
metric itself fails: Chamfer distance under-weights thin
sheets [47], a failure now documented analytically [42] and
addressed by density-aware and information-theoretic variants [30, 47]. We take the further step of pairing geometric metrics against frozen-latent phenotype probes and show
they can rank modalities oppositely.

A.2. Learned parametric recovery of crop plants

578
579
580

Two 2025 systems recover parametric plants directly from
scans. Demeter learns a morphology model (topology, articulation, PCA shape) from real soybean point clouds [8];
Ghrer et al. infer grammar-style parameterized plants from
3D scans [13]. Both take a single, reasonably complete
point cloud as input and neither addresses missing modalities or row occlusion. CropCraft recovers crop structure by
Bayesian optimization of a procedural model against multiview NeRF depth [52]; the approach is accurate but perscene and slow. A staged pipeline annotates terrestrial laser
scans with a vision-language model and fits a NURBS procedural descriptor by differentiable refinement [9, 17, 18]; it
handles isolated, occlusion-free plants at ∼10 minutes each
and serves in this paper as both the occlusion-free baseline
and the source of real-data procedural ground truth. TreeD Fusion reconstructs simulation-ready urban trees from
street imagery via diffusion priors [29], but its envelopethen-branch pipeline targets woody silhouettes, a different
geometry class from thin cereal blades.
