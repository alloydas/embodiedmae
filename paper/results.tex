\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and budgets.}
All ablations share one budget of 197{,}400 optimizer steps at global batch 32
(600 epochs of one view per plant over the 10{,}500 training plants), one
view-sampling seed, and the base ViT (114\,M parameters) unless model size is
the variable; arms that change the number of training plants keep the step
budget by changing their epoch count (Sec.~\ref{sec:pretrain}). The headline
model differs in schedule (1{,}000 epochs over all ten views at global batch
256) and in its point-cloud loss, which adds the Sinkhorn term of
Sec.~\ref{sec:pretrain}. The headline model, like the four-stream ablation arm,
takes the parameter stream as an encoder input under the shared masking budget,
and the teacher and every student of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-distill} are initialized
from its weights. The sorghum headline model and its distillation are on the
final release; \todo{the few headline-derived numbers still from the
first-release, Chamfer-only run are marked where they appear.} A maize headline
model on the same schedule and loss reached 1{,}000 epochs (validation Chamfer
$0.67\times10^{-3}$); its generalist, the three RGB students and two of the
four two-stream students are distilled, and the other two are training.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We measure geometry by the symmetric Chamfer distance in the unit-sphere frame
($\times10^{-3}$; a plant spans $\approx$1.4\,m, so 1.0 is about 7\,mm), computed
on the masked validation step for pretrained arms and with the source unmasked
for students. Parameter error is the mean absolute error on the normalized $[0,1]$
fields of hidden, non-padding leaf tokens; where a per-field number is more
telling we report \emph{skill}, $1-\mathrm{MAE}/\mathrm{MAE}_{\text{mean}}$,
so that 1 is exact and $\le 0$ is no better than the average plant. Phenotype
readout follows Sec.~\ref{sec:field}: ridge regression on the frozen CLS token
with the parameter tensor zeroed, one deterministic view per plant, reported as
$R^2$ on the extreme-enriched held-out plants (2{,}250 val, 2{,}250 test; val
unless stated) with paired bootstrap intervals over plants (2{,}000 resamples).
A difference is a \emph{win} only when its interval excludes zero on val
\emph{and} test. Sorghum's four named traits have rank two by construction
(height and leaf count are one generator variable, $r{=}0.994$, and the biomass
proxy is that variable again), so we add the independent axes varied by the
generator, leaf roll and leaf length. Maize has eleven largely independent
traits (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}). Implementation details, per-trait tables and
intervals are in the supplement.

Table~\ref{tab:regime1} covers deployment on an isolated plant: from one
photograph, one depth frame or one cloud, the model returns the recipe and the
other streams in one forward pass. Depth is the best single input for the cloud
in both species (Chamfer $0.92$ in sorghum and $0.64\times10^{-3}$ in maize, F1
$0.44$ and $0.53$), and the photograph alone comes within a factor of 1.5 of it.
On the full test split the photograph-to-cloud Chamfer is $0.88$ in sorghum and
$0.83$ in maize, with skill $0.98$ and $0.99$ against another plant of the split
(Sec.~\ref{sec:res-distill}). The recipe is recovered from any single sensor: the
leaf count is within one leaf for $91$ to $100\,\%$ of plants and the height
within $2$ to $5\,\%$, and the mean leaf-parameter skill is $0.66$ to $0.68$ in
sorghum and $0.57$ to $0.59$ in maize. The skill is high on the leaf's position
along the stem ($0.93$ to $0.97$ in sorghum, $0.83$ in maize) and on its angle
($0.89$ to $0.95$; $0.76$ to $0.79$), moderate on length ($0.62$ to $0.70$), and
lowest on blade width in sorghum ($0.20$ to $0.22$), whose spread between plants
is small. The headline model given all three streams is no better than the
generalist given one, because it was never trained to read every token of its
input. On the first release, three waviness fields were unrecoverable from any
source (skill $-0.04$ to $0.02$); the final generator does not include them.
Fig.~\ref{fig:qual} shows one held-out plant per species generated from the
photograph alone: the headline model used as is produces a diffuse cloud
around the right silhouette, the distilled generalist recovers the individual
blades, and the leaf lengths it reads off the photograph follow the ground
truth along the stem. \todo{Add the edit demo: change one recovered parameter

\paragraph{Viewpoint.}
The ten cameras of a plant form an elevation ladder
($\sin\text{elev} = -0.9 + 0.2\,i$), and every view shares one cloud and one
recipe, so the view index changes the input but not the target.
Figure~\ref{fig:views}A shows the photograph-to-cloud error by elevation. The
error is U-shaped in elevation, lowest for side-on views and highest for views
looking steeply down \emph{or} up (top-down views are hard, but so are
bottom-up ones). The curve has the same shape for the pretrained model and every
student. Distillation lowers the error by $28\,\%$ on average and improves nine
of ten views, but does not flatten the curve (best-to-worst ratio
$2.35\to2.49$). Across the 2{,}250 test plants the camera's distance from the
horizon explains $\eta^2 = 0.24$--$0.31$ of the per-plant error, and no measure
of the plant itself (leaf count, stem length, slenderness or extent of the
cloud) correlates beyond $|\rho| = 0.06$. In this regime, the view rather than
the morphology determines how accurately a photograph can be inverted. The
recipe is the more view-stable output: the recovered insertion heights of one
plant vary by $0.08$ of the between-leaf spread across its ten photographs, and
the recovered branching angles vary by $0.12$. Distillation halves the error of
both fields ($-59$ and $-60\,\%$). The zero-skill waviness fields did not
improve: their error stayed flat and their view spread grew from $0.18$ to
$0.26$, so a field that no input can predict is not neutral, because it becomes
more view-dependent under distillation.

% reports/probe_pr_e5_d2_17060237.csv, reports/e5_delta_d2_17060238.csv (final release)
\paragraph{Training views.}
We restricted the pretraining draw of the four-stream arm to a band of the
elevation ladder at equal steps: views 0--1 (top-down), views 4--5 (near the
horizon, the height of a tractor-mounted camera) or view 0 alone, against all
ten views. The probe scores view 0 for every arm. Paired over plants against
the all-view arm, the horizon arm loses six of the nine learnable targets on
both splits (test: roll $-0.065$, leaf angle $-0.024$, size $-0.010$ to
$-0.013$). The top-down arm wins three targets and ties on size, and the
single-view arm wins six (test: roll $+0.061$, leaf length $+0.069$, size
$+0.008$). View 0 lies inside the training band of the top-down and single-view
arms and outside the band of the horizon arm, so these differences measure the
overlap between training and test views as much as the breadth of the training
draw. To separate the two, we refit and scored the probe on each view $k$ of
every plant (Fig.~\ref{fig:e5views}).
Each restricted arm leads on roll only on the views it trained on: against the
all-view arm, test roll is $+0.061$ (single), $+0.042$ (top-down) and $+0.027$
(horizon) on the training views and $-0.066$, $-0.059$ and $-0.054$ on average
on the other views, down to $-0.144$ for the single-view arm on view 6. The
all-view arm is the most even, $0.734$ to $0.778$ across the ten views. Size
separates the arms much less: height stays within $0.02$ of the all-view arm on
every view, and the restricted arms lose most on the steepest views (horizon
$-0.013$ on view 0; top-down $-0.019$ and horizon $-0.014$ on view 9, which
looks up at the plant). A narrow band of training views therefore
raises the probe on that band and lowers it elsewhere, so a deployment camera
whose elevation is known in advance favors training at that elevation, and an
unknown one favors the full ladder.

\paragraph{Self-occlusion.}
Training clouds are complete; a rig's are not. In Tab.~\ref{tab:views}, each
model is scored on the points visible to one or three cameras, about half or
three quarters of a sorghum plant. A model that sees RGB, with the probe refit
per condition, loses $0.015$ to $0.03$ of height $R^2$ at one camera and under
$0.01$ at three, against $-0.12$ and $-0.05$ for PC-only. At one camera, roll
loses $0.06$ to $0.11$ and leaf length loses $0.04$ to $0.05$. Depth matters
more on a partial cloud than on a complete one: at one camera, PC+RGB loses
$-0.029\ [-0.033,-0.026]$ on height and PC+RGB+D loses
$-0.020\ [-0.022,-0.017]$. However, the latent representation shifts with
coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:partial}): a readout fitted on complete clouds and
transferred to one-camera clouds collapses (PC+RGB height
$0.977 \to 0.624 \to 0.001$; headline model $0.982 \to 0.623 \to -0.26$), and
refitting recovers $0.948$ and $0.960$. The headline model has the best matched
probe and the best complete-cloud Chamfer of all five models, but its one-camera
Chamfer ($11.6$) is no better than the E2 arms'. The decoder also
does not complete the hidden part of the plant: at one camera, the distance from
the true cloud to the prediction is $11.4$ to $12.8$ against $4.1$ for echoing
the partial input, so the model reproduces what it is given rather than
hallucinating the hidden half. The real-data protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:res-field}
follows from these results: the probe will be refit on scans, and the transfer
gap will be reported as the predicted sim-to-real penalty.

\paragraph{Morphological complexity.}
The held-out splits are enriched with unusual morphologies by construction
(Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), and across them neither reconstruction nor the probe
degrades with plant complexity: the per-plant photograph-to-cloud error is
uncorrelated with leaf count and stem length ($|\rho| \le 0.06$ above), and
the bootstrap intervals in every table are computed over these
extreme-enriched plants. Fig.~\ref{fig:leafcount} shows the same on the
test plants of both species: after distillation, the photograph-to-cloud
error is flat across leaf-count deciles (Spearman $\rho = -0.07$ in sorghum and
$+0.04$ in maize), while the headline model used as is is worse on maize plants
with few leaves ($\rho = -0.28$). \todo{Maize self-occlusion rows of
Tab.~\ref{tab:views}.}

The row data pairs every observation with an isolated twin (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}),
so Tab.~\ref{tab:rows} compares each plant with itself: only the neighbors
change between its isolated and row columns. The table answers three
questions. How much does the zero-shot model lose when a third of its pixels and
two thirds of its cloud belong to other plants (the echo test: Chamfer against
the input cloud indicates whether the model reconstructs the target or the
scene)? Does training against the target plant behind its neighbors recover the
lost accuracy, and does the isolated control, which sees the same plants without
neighbors, improve by the same amount (which would make the gain a data effect,
not an occlusion effect)? And does the error follow the occlusion measure, by
yaw ($13\,\%$ of the target hidden broadside against $1\,\%$ edge-on) and by
quartile? From Sec.~\ref{sec:regime2} we predict that inputs including RGB lose
least and that the decoder will not complete a hidden blade, so the recipe,
which describes the whole plant, should be the least affected output.
Fig.~\ref{fig:rowsex} shows held-out plants of both species in their rows.

\paragraph{What the rows cost.}
On maize (Tab.~\ref{tab:rows}, Fig.~\ref{fig:rows}), neighbors cause a large
error only for a model that was not trained on rows. The isolated control,
fine-tuned from the headline model, is sixteen times worse on rows than on its
own clean inputs ($29.0$ against $1.8\times10^{-4}\,$m$^2$, $+27.2\
[+26.9, +27.6]$), with the leaf-recipe error higher by $0.050$. The headline
model without fine-tuning loses little to the neighbors ($+3.2\ [+2.9, +3.6]$)
because it is already poor under the rover camera, whose viewpoint and
framing it never saw ($26.4$ on the isolated twins). Training against the
target plant behind its neighbors removes $92\,\%$ of the row penalty: the
row-trained model on rows ($4.0$) beats the isolated-trained model on rows by
$-25.0\ [-25.3, -24.7]$, and its error is only $+2.2\ [+2.2, +2.3]$ above the
isolated-trained model on clean inputs, with the leaf-recipe error higher by
$0.008$ and $0.042$ lower than the isolated-trained model on rows. The
first-release models gave the same picture ($91\,\%$). The row-trained model's Chamfer against the input scene rises during
training while its Chamfer against the target falls, so the model reconstructs
the target plant, not the row. Depth alone is the best single input for the
row-trained model ($9.6$), the scene cloud alone is the weakest ($21.9$), and
the error doubles from its minimum at $20$ to $40^\circ$ yaw ($3.2$) to end-on
($6.4$ at $90^\circ$), where the neighbors overlap the target in the image.

Sorghum repeats the result at a larger scale. The isolated-plant model is ten
times worse on rows than on its own clean inputs ($133.8$ against
$13.1\times10^{-4}\,$m$^2$), and row training removes $85\,\%$ of that
penalty: the row-trained model on rows beats the isolated-trained model on rows
by $-103.1\ [-104.3, -102.0]$, and its error is $+17.6\ [+17.4, +17.8]$ above
the isolated-trained model on clean inputs, with the leaf-recipe error higher
by $0.013$. Depth alone is again the best single input ($18.6$). Unlike maize,
the sorghum error does not depend on yaw (Fig.~\ref{fig:rows}d). The zero-shot
headline model is worse than the isolated control even on isolated inputs
($174.3$ against $13.1$), because the row renderer's clouds are partial
captures while the headline model trained on complete clouds; this row is
therefore also a partial-cloud test, as in Tab.~\ref{tab:views}.

Table~\ref{tab:e2} and Fig.~\ref{fig:streams} compare the stream sets at equal
compute. \textbf{An image stream adds most of the phenotype information.} On
sorghum, adding either the photograph or the depth image to the cloud gives the
largest gain in every column (height $0.895\!\to\!0.979$ with RGB and
$0.895\!\to\!0.976$ with depth; roll $0.39\!\to\!0.81$ and
$0.39\!\to\!0.80$), and the depth image wins all nine learnable targets over
the cloud alone on both splits. On maize, the photograph gives the same jump
(height $0.17\!\to\!0.62$, stem radius $0.29\!\to\!0.75$), and the depth
image a smaller one (height $0.17\!\to\!0.45$; nine of eleven traits won, curl
lost). The depth image
records the same surface as the cloud, so its gain does not come from new
information about the plant. A likely reason is that dense, ordered image
patches present the geometry in a form that the encoder reads more easily than
sampled point tokens. Once depth is present, the photograph adds little:
paired over plants, PC+RGB+D wins leaf width ($+0.07$, test) and leaf count
(by $0.001$) against PC+D, loses roll ($-0.015$) and maximum leaf length
($-0.06$), and ties the other five targets.
\textbf{Added to RGB, depth improves reconstruction but not the phenotype
readout:} depth
lowers the Chamfer by $28\,\%$ in sorghum and by $25\,\%$ in maize. On sorghum,
it changes the probe by thousandths: paired over plants, it loses on size
($-0.002$ to $-0.004$) and on the two angles, and gains only on leaf length
($+0.05$). On maize (final release, paired over plants), depth wins no trait and
loses three on both splits, namely tassel droop ($-0.10$ to $-0.12$), curl
($-0.06$ to $-0.10$) and twist ($-0.03$), and the other eight traits tie within
$0.015$.
\textbf{The parameter stream, used as an input, does not help.} On sorghum the
four-stream arm loses to PC+RGB+D on all nine learnable targets on both splits
(size $-0.015$ to $-0.018$, branch $-0.038$, roll $-0.017$, leaf length
$-0.075$, leaf width $-0.14$; every interval excludes zero), and its Chamfer,
$2.53$ against $1.85$, loses the improvement that depth brought. The other
three sorghum stream sets show the same pattern. Added to PC+D, the parameter
stream loses all nine targets; added to PC+RGB, it loses seven and ties roll and
maximum leaf length; added to the cloud alone, it wins roll ($+0.10$) and loses
the three size traits ($-0.03$). It raises the Chamfer in every set that
contains an image stream ($2.45\to4.08$ with depth, $2.57\to3.00$ with RGB)
and lowers it only for the cloud alone ($5.00\to4.84$). On the first
maize release, the same arm lost 10 of 11 traits. On the final release, the
paired comparison is a draw: PC+RGB+D wins droop ($+0.04$ to $+0.07$), loses
curl ($-0.04$ to $-0.07$) and twist ($-0.035$), and ties the eight remaining
traits within $0.02$ (height $0.635$ vs.\ $0.633$, stem radius $0.743$ vs.\
$0.760$), while the four-stream arm has the better Chamfer. Against PC+RGB, the
four-stream arm loses droop ($-0.05$) and tassel droop ($-0.13$) and ties the
rest. The new maize arms do not support a uniform rule either: added to the
cloud alone, the parameter stream wins seven traits and loses none, added to
PC+RGB it wins three (twist, curl, tassel droop) and loses five, and added to
PC+D it wins three and loses two. Either way, the stream that encodes the answer verbatim in training is not
the stream that makes the frozen feature better at deployment, where it is
absent.

A fourth stream at mask ratio $0.8$ splits the visible budget four ways, so
the comparison in Tab.~\ref{tab:e2} alone cannot separate a token-budget
handicap from a representational one: as trained, the four-stream arm's sensors
see $107.4$ visible tokens against $117.0$, and its parameter tokens are
$58\,\%$ visible. In the control arms of Tab.~\ref{tab:control}, the handicap
is removed. Restoring the sensor budget does not recover the three-stream arm's
probe scores at any parameter visibility; the trend with visibility is monotone
on the size targets ($60\,\% < 20\,\% < 0\,\%$ visible), and making the
parameters a pure reconstruction target still loses $0.011$ on size, $0.07$ on
roll, $0.10$ on leaf length and $0.15$ on leaf width. The per-leaf traits stored
in the parameter tokens (roll, leaf length and leaf width) lose the most. The
pattern is consistent with the encoder routing these traits through tokens that
do not exist in the field, and the target-only result shows that the
reconstruction pressure alone accounts for part of the loss.

Maize gives the opposite result for the gated arms (Tab.~\ref{tab:control}).
With the sensor budget restored, the arm that sees 20\,\% of the parameter
tokens and the arm that only reconstructs them beat both the shared-budget arm
and PC+RGB+D on all eleven traits on both splits (target only, val: height
$0.776$ against $0.633$, stem radius $0.804$ against $0.760$), while the arm
that sees 60\,\% loses nine traits. Making most of the parameter stream visible
to the encoder therefore hurts in both species, but a parameter target hurts the
sorghum representation and helps the maize one. The headline models were
nevertheless trained with the parameters in the encoder's shared budget
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pretrain}), as in the four-stream arm of Tab.~\ref{tab:e2}. For
sorghum, the ablations predict that the headline model's frozen representation
is weaker than that of a three-stream or $\rho_\theta{=}1$ model; for maize,
they predict the opposite of $\rho_\theta{=}1$. We have not tested either at the
headline scale. Supervising the parameters as a target, in turn, lets
the generalist beat the parameter specialist (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-distill}).

\label{sec:res-metrics}

\begin{figure}[t]

% figures/make_metric_fig.py from the tables above
Chamfer and the probe rank the arms alike only in the coarse sense
(Fig.~\ref{fig:metrics}; Spearman $-0.57$ to $-0.88$ across twelve arms), and
they disagree on every design decision in this paper.
Depth is the clearest case: $-28\,\%$ Chamfer in sorghum, $-25\,\%$ in maize,
and a probe that does not move (Sec.~\ref{sec:param-stream}). Data is the
second: sorghum Chamfer keeps improving from 1{,}000 to 10{,}000 plants
($-21\,\%$) while the size targets stay within $0.93$--$0.96$ and are not
monotone (Tab.~\ref{tab:scaling}), and in maize Chamfer falls fourfold while the
probe is not even monotone. The parameter stream is the third: on the final
maize release it gives the best Chamfer of the four arms and no probe gain. And
the partial clouds of Tab.~\ref{tab:views} are the fourth, in the other
direction: at one camera Chamfer is $2.6$ to $6.5\times$ worse while the refit probe
loses at most $0.03$ on height. Point-MAE, an external baseline, beats our PC-only arm on every sorghum trait
under the probe (Tab.~\ref{tab:e8}), so our advantage comes from fusing a
photograph with geometry, not from a better cloud encoder.

A nearest-neighbor distance is minimized by surface proximity and allows many
predicted points to match one target point, so it rewards a decoder that places
points on the bulk of a slender plant and barely penalizes a missing or merged
blade~\cite{wu2021dcd,song2026chamfer}. The probe instead measures the
structure retained by the encoder, which is the input to a trait pipeline. We
therefore use the probe to decide between modality and data choices and report
Chamfer as the decoder's surface error. On the training side, we add the
Sinkhorn term of Sec.~\ref{sec:pretrain}, which matches the two clouds as
distributions and is not minimized by a clump of points. \todo{Add the E7 ablation of that term, and
F-score at 1\,cm beside Chamfer in Tabs.~\ref{tab:regime1} and
\ref{tab:anysubset} (student F-scores queued).}

\paragraph{F-score.}
Precision and recall at a distance threshold expose the failure hidden by
Chamfer (Fig.~\ref{fig:fscore}). For every model, precision is far higher than
recall: the headline model places $51\,\%$ of its points within 1\,cm of the
plant but covers only $32\,\%$ of the plant's points (F1 $0.39$; $0.72$ at
2\,cm). The reconstruction is accurate where it places points but misses whole
regions, namely the thin blades that a nearest-neighbor distance barely
penalizes. The ablation arms rank the same under F1 as under Chamfer, so F1 adds
an interpretable unit but does not change any decision.

\paragraph{Against published encoders.}
In Tab.~\ref{tab:e8}, four public checkpoints and a supervised network are
read through the same probe. On sorghum the three-stream \CropCode{} beats every
published encoder on every trait in Tab.~\ref{tab:e8}; against the strongest,
EmbodiedMAE-B, it wins eight of nine paired over plants: $+0.004$ to $+0.007$
on size (tight intervals at a $0.98$ ceiling), $+0.03$ on branch, $+0.04$ on
roll, $+0.08$ on leaf length and $+0.09$ on leaf width. The four-stream arm
loses the size traits to EmbodiedMAE-B by $0.008$ to $0.011$: the
parameter-stream penalty of Sec.~\ref{sec:param-stream} measured against an
external model. A network of our architecture trained from scratch on the probe
targets reaches $0.974$ height and $0.767$ roll through its own head; the
frozen three-stream feature beats it on seven of nine traits and loses none
(paired, CLS token; under mean pooling the supervised network wins branch),
while the frozen four-stream feature loses size and branch. Pretraining helps
without the parameter stream; with it, the frozen feature is slightly worse than
training from scratch. On the final maize release (val, point estimates), the
four-stream arm beats EmbodiedMAE-B on 10 of 11 traits and the best baseline per
trait without camera pose on 9, losing leaf count to Point-MAE ($0.908$ against
$0.926$) and tassel droop to DINOv2 ($-0.048$ against $0.297$). The maize
headline model beats every such baseline on 10 of 11 (height $0.739$ against
$0.570$, curl $0.698$ against $0.199$, stem radius $0.830$ against $0.588$) and
still loses tassel droop to DINOv2 ($0.215$ against $0.297$), the trait where
every encoder of the EmbodiedMAE design is weakest. Unlike sorghum, the maize
network trained from scratch on the probe targets is ahead of every frozen
600-epoch arm on all eleven traits (mean pooled: height $0.785$, curl $0.618$),
and the maize headline model closes most of that gap and passes it on curl (height $0.739$, curl
$0.698$ on the CLS token).

\paragraph{Camera pose, a forensic note.}
Point-MAE given each view's full camera pose beats our camera-frame four-stream
arm on 8 of 11 maize traits (height $0.720$ vs.\ $0.597$). Most of this
advantage is a generator artifact: the maize renderer puts every plant's leaf
plane in the same world plane, so a full pose rotation gives the encoder a
canonical plant azimuth that no real rig provides. With gravity only (the camera
leveled and its azimuth kept, the information a calibrated rig provides),
Point-MAE's height falls to $0.588$ and camera-frame PC+RGB+D wins five traits
(height, biomass, twist, width, stem radius $+0.23$) and loses four (leaf angle,
leaf count, curl $-0.18$, tassel droop $-0.21$). Our fused arms do not improve
with leveled clouds, and the PC-only arm improves on five traits. A generator's
unintended regularities are a benchmark hazard, and this one was found only
because the baseline scored suspiciously well.

\paragraph{Across species.}
We pretrained the three-stream model (PC+RGB+D) on each species for 150

\paragraph{What the latent encodes.}
% reports/e9_pr_600_16592054/, reports/e9_pr_tg100_600_16686383/, reports/e9_pm_600_22751615/
There is no genotype in either generator: every plant is one draw from one
continuous distribution, so a clustered latent would be a defect, not a
finding. The frozen CLS space is a continuum (best silhouette $0.15$ to $0.24$
at $k{=}2$, adjusted Rand index $\approx 0$ against shape deciles, t-SNE
trustworthiness $0.95$ to $0.99$) of low effective dimension: participation
ratio is $4.4$ to $6.3$ (of 768) in sorghum and $6.7$ to $11.3$ in maize. Once
RGB is present, the number of principal components needed for $90\,\%$ of the
variance is 14 to 15 in sorghum and 18 to 19 in maize, consistent with maize's
more independent generative factors. In sorghum, the leading latent direction is
the plant's size (first component vs.\ leaf count $|r| = 0.73$ to $0.94$ across
the arms); in maize, no single component aligns with a trait (best
single-component correlation with leaf count $|r| \le 0.17$ in every arm,
although the probe reads leaf count at $R^2 = 0.91$ from the same features), so
the maize traits are spread over many directions. The parameter stream changes
the sorghum latent geometry in the way the probe results predict: the gated arms
are the least aligned with size ($|r|$ $0.48$ and $0.34$) and the most isotropic
(participation ratio $8.5$ and $14.3$), and the target-only arm is the most
dominated by size of the five (first component $43\,\%$ of variance,
$|r| = 0.95$). Linear decodability is also distinct from local embedding
structure: the probe reads roll at $R^2$ $0.81$ from a latent in which roll is
barely organized locally, so we use the probe, not t-SNE smoothness, as the
measure for representation claims on a continuous generator
(Fig.~\ref{fig:e9}).
