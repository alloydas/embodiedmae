Cross-modal generation, before and after distillation

Given one modality and nothing else, the model reconstructs the other three. Distillation against a frozen full-modal teacher improves that by 19.1 % overall — and by more than half where the model started weakest. Below: that result, the modality ablation, the data- and model-scaling curves that both closed this week, and the first numbers from the decision-6.4 linear probe and the E9 latent analysis — which disagree with chamfer about what depth is worth. New: the token-budget control arms (the budget was not the cause), the same experiments on maize, a second species whose four 6.4 targets are genuinely independent, and the E8 baselines: four frozen public encoders through the same probe. New on 30 Sep: the supervised-from-scratch row (pretraining pays, but not with the parameter stream as an input), the maize E2 ablation (the stream hurts maize too), and one camera vs three: the same frozen models on the partial clouds a camera rig would capture. New on 1 Oct: parameters as a target only (still worse than leaving them out), and camera pose, one step at a time: the maize Point-MAE + pose lead is largely the generator's fixed plant azimuth, and against gravity alone our model wins 5 targets and loses 4.

Where it helps most is where it was worst

RGB and depth were already decent conditioning signals and gain about 11 %. The two weak sources gain far more: conditioning on point cloud alone improves 27.5 %, and PC chamfer from the spline parameters alone drops 61.9 %. Distillation is not lifting the whole curve uniformly — it is closing the gap between the strong and weak entry points, which is exactly what the frozen full-modal teacher is there to do.

Parameter regression follows the same shape: param_mae from a point cloud falls 43.8 % (0.0617 → 0.0347), against roughly 9–10 % from RGB or depth.

The generalist beats the parameter specialist at its own job

Distilling only the parameter head gives −36.9 %. Distilling all four modalities at once gives −43.8 % on that same parameter metric — 11 % better than the run that optimised for nothing else. The other three reconstruction targets are acting as useful auxiliary supervision for parameter regression rather than competing with it.

One honest caveat before this goes in a slide: the two runs were trained at different learning rates (3e−5 vs 1e−4), so part of that gap could be LR rather than the target set. A matched-LR pc2text run would settle it, and it is cheap — 100 epochs on a single source.

Views

Ten images of one plant

The ten folders of a plant are not arbitrary: the view index is an exact elevation ladder. Carrying world-up through each view's worldToCamera gives a component along the camera's forward axis of precisely 0.9 − 0.2·index, so the viewing direction's elevation is −0.9 + 0.2·index — steeply down at view 00, through side-on in the middle, to steeply up at view 09 — while azimuth is randomised. Every view shares one point cloud — _nc_cam.ply is the same geometry rotated into the view frame, and the loader centres and unit-scales before Chamfer — and one set of spline parameters. The answer never moves; only the input image does.

Reconstruction quality is set by camera elevation, and distillation lowers the curve without flattening it

Both models trace the same U: best near side-on (view 06, sin elev 0.12) and worst at both extremes — looking steeply down (view 00) and steeply up (views 08–09). Since the target is identical from every view, that shape is caused entirely by what the camera can see.

Distillation improves 9 of 10 views, mean chamfer 0.001170 → 0.000837 (−28.5 %). But the best-to-worst ratio goes 2.35× → 2.49×: it moves the whole curve down and does not reduce the view dependence. If view robustness is wanted, distillation as configured is not the lever.

The zero-skill fields got noisier, not better

The four fields that carry real skill improved on both counts — starting_point and branching_angle roughly halve their error and become more view-stable. The three waviness fields did the opposite: accuracy unchanged or slightly worse, while their view spread rose from 0.18–0.20 to 0.26.

The parametric modality loses to PC + RGB + depth on this metric

RGB buys 49 %, depth buys a further 16 points, and adding the procedural-parameter stream returns to roughly the PC+RGB level. All four arms have since run the full 600 epochs, so this is a settled gap rather than one arm being behind.

There are two readings, and they have different consequences. Mechanical: at a fixed mask_ratio of 0.80 with min_mask_ratio 0.25 per modality, a fourth token stream splits the visible-token budget four ways instead of three — the PC decoder simply sees less to work from, and chamfer penalises the params arm for existing rather than for being unhelpful. Real: the parametric modality genuinely does not help the representation.

Chamfer cannot separate these two. The downstream linear probe can — and the submission plan's locked decision 6.4 already names that probe, not reconstruction loss, as the metric that settles modality value-add. This is now the decisive piece of missing tooling, not a documentation gap.

e4_large spent its last day closing on base — 32.9 % behind at epoch 250, 19.5 % at epoch 350, level at epoch 550 — and then turned: chamfer rose to 0.002504 at epoch 575 and 0.002532 at epoch 600. It reached base and never passed it.

Capacity pays once, then stops

25 M → 114 M is worth −17.1 %. 114 M → 332 M — nearly triple again — is worth nothing measurable, on chamfer (+0.3 %) or on parameter accuracy (+0.3 pt), both inside the noise floor. The model-scaling curve is a step, not a slope, and base is the knee.

Both axes flatten at the top: more plants past ~10,000 and more parameters past 114 M each buy about as much as re-running the same job. At 197,400 steps neither is the binding constraint — which points at the step budget itself, and makes compute the one axis E3 and E4 never varied.

A node event took two arms, and the resume logic had to be fixed

e3_1k and e4_large both died at the same second on 19 Sep on the preemptible scavenger partition — no traceback, host memory far under request. Auto-resume then picked e4_large’s newest checkpoint, which had been truncated mid-write by the kill, and the job failed at torch.load in 2 min 45 s. The launcher now walks down to the newest checkpoint that actually opens; both arms were re-queued on the non-preemptible partition and ran to completion from there.

The parametric stream loses on every target — and chamfer said so too

Adding the procedural-parameter stream is worse than PC+RGB+depth on all eight targets, not tied: height 0.981 → 0.966, leaf count 0.984 → 0.967, biomass 0.986 → 0.969, leaf length 0.362 → 0.278. Chamfer said the same (0.001706 → 0.002478). Two independent metrics, same direction, and the probe is the one decision 6.4 named.

But this does not resolve the question the E2 section poses, and I had claimed it would. The probe removes the probe-time confound completely — both arms are scored on an identical 589-token input with text excluded, so nothing differs at evaluation. The token-budget handicap lives in pretraining: at mask_ratio 0.80 a fourth stream splits the visible budget four ways, so RGB, depth and PC each saw fewer tokens for 600 epochs. The probe cannot see back into that.

One control arm separates them, and it is the highest-value run left: re-run the four-modality arm at a mask ratio giving RGB/depth/PC the same visible-token count they get in the three-modality arm. Still loses → the parametric modality genuinely does not help and the novelty claim needs rethinking. Catches up → E2 is a budget artefact and the claim survives. One 600-epoch run at the shared 197,400-step budget. Now run — it still loses; see the control arms below.

Depth is the big win on chamfer and a rounding error on phenotype

E2 ranks depth as the largest single gain: PC+RGB → PC+RGB+D takes chamfer from 0.002504 to 0.001706, −32 %. On the metric 6.4 actually specifies, the same step moves height 0.978 → 0.981 and leaf count 0.983 → 0.984 — inside the noise. RGB is where the phenotype information arrives (+0.08 R² on every real axis); depth refines the surface without telling you more about the plant.

So the modality ranking depends on which metric you believe, and 6.4 already chose. The one place depth clearly pays is leaf_len_mean: 0.274 → 0.362, the only target still far from saturation.

Three of decision 6.4's four targets are the same measurement

The generator sets stem_length = 0.05 × n_leaves − 0.001 (R² 0.988), so height and leaf count are one variable (r = 0.994). The biomass proxy n_leaves × leaf_len_mean is r = 0.996 with leaf count — the same variable a third time. The four-target table has rank 2, and the three near-identical R² values in each column above are one result printed three times.

This is a property of the data generator, not of the probe, and it does not weaken the E2 comparison — every arm is scored on the same targets. But the paper cannot present four independent phenotypes recovered. The genuinely independent axes are leaf length and roll angle.

Roll angle is the most informative target, having looked like the least

roll_mean cannot be predicted from the other tabular columns at all (oracle linear R² 0.0009) — it is the mean of n near-uniform circular draws. That made it look like a dead target. It is not: the latent is not the tabular columns, it sees the leaves, and the probe recovers roll at R² 0.806. It is also the only target essentially uncorrelated with the size axis (r = 0.012 with leaf count) and it separates the arms hardest — 0.449 → 0.806, the widest spread in the table.

The probe cannot cheat, by construction

param_floats row 0, element 0 is stem_length — the height target, verbatim. An arm allowed to see the spline stream at probe time would be handed the answer and the four-modality arm would "win" E2 on a leak. The probe excludes text from the visible set and feeds zeros in the param slot; the latent is bit-identical either way (max|diff| = 0.0, verified), so the leak is structurally impossible rather than merely avoided. A useful side effect: PC+RGB+D and +params are probed on an identical 589-token input, so the only difference between them is what pretraining taught the encoder.

Restoring the budget does not recover the gap

With vision back at exactly the three-modality arm's 117 tokens, the parameter arm does not catch up. The text-at-0.80 arm lands at 0.958 on height and 0.764 on roll, slightly below the arm as trained, and far from PC + RGB + depth's 0.981 and 0.806. Chamfer says the same. The mechanical reading is ruled out for these arms: the gap in the E2 table is not an artefact of the token budget.

The more parameter text the encoder sees, the worse its latent — but seeing none of it costs roll

The two gated arms with text visible have the same vision budget and differ only in text visibility. At 20 % visible: height 0.958, roll 0.764, leaf length 0.288. At 60 %: 0.922, 0.676, 0.202, and chamfer rises from 0.00241 to 0.00272. Seeing more of the parameters during pretraining makes the frozen image/geometry latent worse on every target with skill, with CIs clear of zero on both splits for all of them except maximum leaf length (a loss on test, a tie on val).

Taking the text away entirely (tg100, 0 % visible) continues the trend on size: +0.012 to +0.014 over the 20 % arm on height, leaf count and biomass, both splits, and branch angle gains on test (+0.027; val a tie). On roll it reverses, −0.027 / −0.031 (val / test), and mean leaf length is a val-only loss (−0.028; test +0.008, a tie). So the visibility trend is clean on size; on roll it reverses.

One reading, not yet tested directly: the parameter tokens carry the answer (the plant token holds stem length, the height target, verbatim), so when they are visible the encoder can reconstruct from them instead of learning it from the images and the cloud — a shortcut. The probe removes the text, so whatever the encoder learned to take from it is gone. The E9 geometry agrees: leaf-count alignment of the latent falls from |r| 0.76 (PC + RGB + depth) and 0.73 (as trained) to 0.48 (text 0.80) and 0.34 (text 0.40).

A caveat on the finer decomposition: the 0.40 arm also differs from the arm as trained in how text is masked (independently rather than inside one Dirichlet draw) and runs 8 % more encoder tokens, so "0.40 vs as-trained" is not a clean single-variable comparison. The 0.80 vs 0.40 comparison is.

Parameters as a target only: still below PC + RGB + depth on every learnable target

The direct test of "target, not input" is the four-modality arm with text masked at 1.0: the parameters are never visible to the encoder and are only reconstructed by the decoder (e2_pcrgbdt_tg100, same 197,400-step budget). Had it matched PC + RGB + depth, the stream would add value as supervision. It does not. Against PC + RGB + depth, with CIs clear of zero on both splits (val / test):

  • Size (height, leaf count, biomass): −0.010 to −0.012
  • Branch angle −0.028 / −0.016, roll −0.069 / −0.077
  • Mean leaf length −0.102 / −0.097, maximum −0.062 / −0.040

So the reconstruction target costs the frozen latent too, not only the input. Against the arm as trained it recovers a little size (+0.004 to +0.006, both splits) and loses more roll (−0.056 / −0.063). The sorghum leaf tokens include each leaf's length and roll angle, the two targets the latent loses most on: predicting them through the decoder did not make them more decodable from the frozen CLS. One candidate explanation, not tested: each leaf's roll angle is encoded linearly although it is circular, so two near-identical leaves can sit a full range apart at the 0°/360° seam and the per-leaf target is ill-posed there.

E9 shows where the latent went. tg100's latent is the most size-dominated of the five arms: PC1 carries 43 % of the variance and is the size factor (|r| 0.95 with leaf count, where PC + RGB + depth has size on PC3 at 0.76), and its effective rank, 4.6, is the lowest of the five. In sorghum the parameter stream does not improve the frozen latent in any form tested: as an input at any visibility, or as a target alone. Its value is as a generation target (PC → parameters, −43.8 % after distillation). One seed per arm.

E9

Latent analysis — a continuum, and its main axis is not the plant

E9 is specified as "clustering, t-SNE/UMAP by genotype and shape". Half of that has nothing to run against: every plant comes from SorghumGenerator/Random.sg with Seed = plant id — one generator, one continuous distribution, 15,000 draws. The seed is an identifier, not a cultivar, so there is no genotype variable and k-means here would cut a continuum into arbitrary pieces and report the pieces.

That makes the answerable question a stronger one: does the latent recover the generative factors, and is it continuous? A good encoder of a continuous generator should show low cluster separation and high factor alignment — so a near-zero silhouette here is a positive result, which is why it has to be said before the number is read.

The dominant latent direction is not the phenotype

In both RGB arms the size factor lives on PC3, not PC1 — and PC1 still carries ~30 % of all variance. Camera view is ruled out by construction (every plant is scored on its fixed view 00). What PC1 encodes is unknown, and it is the most interesting open question E9 has produced: a third of the representation is spent on something that is not the plant's shape.

E9 corroborates the probe independently. The four-modality arm has the weakest shape alignment of any arm (|r| 0.64 against 0.76 and 0.94) and the weakest shape-distance correlation (0.215). Two unrelated analyses — a supervised ridge and an unsupervised look at the geometry — agree that its latent carries less about the plant.

The latent is also remarkably low-dimensional — 4 to 7 effective directions out of 768 — and adding modalities raises that rank (4.4 → 6.5) while lowering PC1's share. More modalities spread the representation out rather than concentrating it.

What the picture shows and what the probe can decode are different questions

Measuring how much each factor varies between t-SNE neighbours against random pairs (1.0 = no local structure, 0 = perfectly smooth): leaf count 0.17, roll angle 0.79, leaf length 0.91, extremeness 0.91, waviness 0.98. Only leaf count is strongly organised in the embedding. Roll is weakly organised — clearly above waviness, nowhere near leaf count — yet the probe decodes it at R² 0.806.

So a direction can be far more linearly decodable than it is locally clustered: t-SNE preserves neighbourhoods, not linear axes. Worth stating in the paper, because a near-flat panel otherwise reads as "the model did not learn it." Waviness, by contrast, is flat on both measures — 0.98 here and R² 0.00 at the probe — which is the genuine negative.

The same experiments on a second species

15,000 procedurally generated maize plants × 10 views, split 70/15/15 by plant — a different generator, with its own dataset, model and probe code, kept separate from sorghum's. Everything else is held to the sorghum protocol: the same 197,400-step budget for every arm, global batch 32, and the same probe, with each arm probed at its last checkpoint of the matched schedule rather than best_model.pth.

The small-data arms overfit — far harder than in sorghum

Maize's 1,000-plant arm reaches its best chamfer at 29 % of its schedule, then gets 79 % worse by the end. The 3,000-plant arm peaks at 54 % and ends 27 % worse. Sorghum's 1,000-plant arm only flattened (−4.7 % over its second half). Here the small-data arms actively degrade.

The fixed-compute rule probes them at the end, so the 1k and 3k probe rows describe overfit encoders. Even so, 1k is within 0.03 R² of 10k on leaf count, biomass and twist. The decoder's geometry degrades far more than the CLS token's phenotype content does. That is an inference, not a measurement: 1k has not been probed at its best-chamfer checkpoint (epoch 1,799), and that single run would show whether the overfitting reaches the latent.

Selecting on validation loss would mislead too. maize_4m's validation loss bottoms out at epoch 300 and rises afterwards, because the parameter-reconstruction term overfits in every arm (it ends 1.1–1.45× above its minimum). Its best_model.pth is therefore a half-trained model. This is the matched-checkpoint rule again, and it holds for maize as well.

So in both species the procedural-parameter stream, as an encoder input during pretraining, costs phenotype information in the frozen latent. Maize is where that could have gone the other way: its four 6.4 targets are independent, and two of them sit verbatim in the plant token. It did not.

RGB is where maize phenotype arrives; depth is a wash

The PC-only arm is far behind on every target but curl. Its height is 0.18 against 0.60–0.65 with RGB, and its leaf count 0.71 against 0.89–0.91. Adding RGB is the step that matters in maize, as in sorghum. Depth on top of PC + RGB moves targets both ways. It helps leaf droop (+0.040 / +0.068), twist, width and count. It costs stem radius (−0.030 / −0.026), and height on val only. Most of these moves are inside the 0.02 floor. That matches sorghum, where depth was the big chamfer win and a rounding error on phenotype.

E9 reads the latents differently from the probe. The three arms without the stream have no principal component aligned with any trait above |r| 0.14, against up to 0.58 for maize_4m, yet they decode better. The trait information is there, spread across many directions rather than lined up with a few. That is the maize pattern in the E9 section below, taken further.

A continuum again — but higher-dimensional, and not aligned to its axes

Exception 2 — Point-MAE is the better point-cloud encoder

Off-the-shelf Point-MAE, pretrained on ShapeNet objects, beats our PC-only arm on every learnable sorghum target on both splits.   • Its sorghum clouds are rotated upright by a constant that our arms never get. It is the same for every plant, so it carries no per-plant information.

Our PC-only arm is even below the untrained network on both leaf-length targets (0.106 vs 0.168 mean length), though that network also sees RGB and depth. So our lead over every baseline comes from fusing RGB with geometry, not from a stronger point-cloud encoder.

Exception 3 — given camera pose, Point-MAE leads on maize, but much of that lead is the generator's plant orientation

Even in the tilted camera frame it was never trained on, Point-MAE already beats maize_4m on leaf count (0.921 vs 0.907) and curl (0.199 vs 0.150), significant on both splits. Rotating each cloud into the renderer's world frame with that view's full camera pose makes it beat maize_4m on eight targets, both splits. Test Δ, ours minus Point-MAE:

  • Height −0.115, leaf angle −0.080, leaf count −0.056, biomass −0.025
  • Droop −0.141, curl −0.278, leaf length −0.097, tassel droop −0.351

That rotation hands over more than gravity. The maize generator places every plant at the same azimuth. Maize leaves are distichous, and the leaf plane is the same world plane for every plant: axial resultant 0.993 over 194 val plants, where 1 is identical and 0 uniform. So the full pose also tells Point-MAE which way the plant faces, which no camera rig knows. Levelling each camera instead, keeping its own azimuth, hands over only the up axis (resultant 0.114), which is what a calibrated rig knows. Point-MAE then lands in between, for example height 0.509 → 0.588 → 0.714 and curl 0.199 → 0.309 → 0.417, and the full pose adds a significant gain on top of gravity on all 11 targets.

Against the realistic baseline, Point-MAE + gravity, PC + RGB + depth with no pose at all wins 5 targets and loses 4. It wins height, biomass, twist, leaf width and stem radius, loses leaf angle, leaf count, curl and tassel droop, and ties droop and leaf length. maize_4m wins 4 and loses 5. Tables and CIs are below.

Tassel droop is readable from the image (DINOv2 0.275) and from a pose-rotated cloud (Point-MAE 0.183 with gravity, 0.309 with the full pose). Every encoder of the EmbodiedMAE design sits below zero: our arms, EmbodiedMAE-B itself (−0.010) and the untrained network. It is our largest single loss and an open question.

Partial clouds

One camera, three cameras or the whole plant: what a partial point cloud costs

Every model in this report trained on the complete plant cloud: each view's _nc_cam.ply is the whole plant moved into that camera's frame, back and inside included. A camera rig sees less. This scores the same frozen sorghum models on the cloud camera 00 alone would capture, and on what cameras 00, 01 and 02 together would capture. Evaluation only: nothing was retrained or fine-tuned.

The three cameras all sit above the plant. Camera 00 looks down at 64°, 01 at 44° from 88° around, and 02 at 30° from 138° the other way. A point counts as seen if a ray from the camera to its nearest spot on the plant's source mesh is not blocked more than 0.5 mm early, and it falls inside the camera's 40° field of view. The pose files carry no intrinsics, so the field of view was fitted from the silhouettes. Checked against the renderer, seen points land on camera 00's silhouette 95 % of the time and match its depth map within 5 mm 71 % of the time, against 3 % for occluded points. RGB and depth stay camera 00's in every condition, so only the cloud changes. Each partial cloud goes through the loader's own steps (subsampled to 8,196 points, centred on its own centroid, scaled to the unit sphere), which is what a deployed model would be handed.

A readout fitted on complete clouds breaks on partial ones

Applied to partial clouds, a probe fitted on complete ones falls apart: the headline model's height R² goes 0.983 → 0.690 at three cameras and −0.21 at one, worse than predicting the mean. The information has not gone, since refitting recovers 0.966. What changes is where the latent puts a plant: a cloud with half its points gone lands somewhere else in the embedding, so a readout calibrated on complete clouds is biased on partial ones.

That matters for anything trained on complete synthetic clouds and used on real scans, E10 included. Either fit the readout on partial clouds or pretrain on them. The four-modality arm transfers best (0.764 at one camera), and nothing here says why; with one seed per arm, do not lean on it.

Discussion

What I need a decision on

  1. The linear probe is built — decide what it is allowed to claim

    Decision 6.4 makes the value-add metric a linear probe on height, leaf angle, leaf count and biomass — explicitly not reconstruction loss. It now exists (eval/linear_probe.py, three of four E2 arms scored). What needs deciding is what it may claim: three of the four named targets are one variable, so the paper cannot report four independent phenotypes, and the probe ranks the modalities differently from chamfer. It is also the only thing that can tell us whether the E2 params result is a token-budget artefact or real. Three of the four targets are already in features.csv; biomass is in neither features.csv nor the spline parameters and needs a definition, and “leaf angle” is ambiguous between roll_angle (twist) and branching_angle (insertion). Every arm the probe needs is now on disk and nothing is training, so for the first time it is not competing with a run for GPUs.

  2. E8 is run — decide which comparison the paper leads with

    Four frozen public encoders are now probed on both species (E8 above). The three-modality sorghum arm beats all of them on every learnable target, and maize_4m beats EmbodiedMAE-B on 9 of 11. But the four-modality sorghum arm loses the size targets to EmbodiedMAE-B, Point-MAE is the better point-cloud encoder, and on maize a Point-MAE given each view's full camera pose beats maize_4m on 8 of 11 targets. Much of that is the generator's fixed plant azimuth, which the full pose hands over. With gravity alone, PC + RGB + depth beats Point-MAE on 5 targets and loses 4 (pose rows). Two decisions follow. First, whether the paper leads with the fused latent (which wins) or the parameter stream (which does not, as an input or as a target alone: tg100). Second, how to report pose. The full-pose row should be captioned as carrying the plant's orientation, and an arm pretrained on levelled clouds is training (decided 1 Oct) so that the gravity comparison can be shown rather than argued. The supervised-from-scratch row is now in, and it sharpens the first decision: frozen PC + RGB + depth beats it (+0.006 on size, +0.08 to +0.12 on leaf length), while the four-modality arm falls below it on size, branch angle and roll (details).

  3. Partial clouds — decide whether pretraining should see them

    Every model here trained on complete plant clouds, which no camera rig captures (one camera vs three). With the probe refitted on partial clouds, the RGB arms lose only 0.01–0.03 R² on size at one camera, so the E2 conclusions survive. But a readout fitted on complete clouds collapses on partial ones (the headline model's height goes 0.983 → −0.21 at one camera), and the decoder does not fill in unseen geometry. If the paper claims transfer to real scans (E10) or shape completion, pretraining or the readout has to see partial clouds. The visibility masks already exist for every plant, cameras 00–02, so training on them is cheap to set up.

  4. Confirm the shared protocol with whoever is running E6 and E7

    Masking/noise and the loss study are being run elsewhere, but their numbers land in the same table as ours. The comparison only holds if they match on three things: the same 70/15/15 split of Sorghum_15K at seed 42, the same global batch of 32, and a budget stated in optimizer steps rather than epochs. The last one is the likeliest to diverge, because it only bites when epoch sizes differ.
